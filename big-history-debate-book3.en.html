Issue 3 · Themed Reading

The Sapiens Quartet

Harari has sold thirty million copies — but is the "story of humanity" he tells the right one? Four books that together stage a debate about how to write big history.

2026 · Bidaily Books · Issue 3

Why these four together

In 2011 Harari almost single-handedly redefined the genre of popular big history with Sapiens — three revolutions (cognitive, agricultural, scientific), humans cooperating through shared fictions, civilization as a sequence of devouring victories. It reads smoothly; the narrative is uninterrupted. But the smoother a big-history book reads, the more it is worth distrusting: smoothness usually means the detail has been deleted.

This issue puts Harari's two flagship books beside the most thoroughgoing rebuttal available — The Dawn of Everything, by anthropologists David Graeber and David Wengrow, whose central charge is that the Harari/Diamond/Pinker generation of big-history writers tells a good story but is badly out of step with thirty years of archaeological and anthropological evidence. We close with Boorstin's The Discoverers as a third way of writing big history altogether: not "what we are," but "how we came to know."

You won't walk away with "the truth about humanity." You will walk away knowing the cost of each of the dominant grand narratives currently on offer.

The four books in detail

Sapiens: A Brief History of Humankind
Yuval Noah Harari
2011 Hebrew / 2014 English · Harper · ~450 pages
One of the most influential works of non-fiction of the past decade. Concede that first, then criticize.
Core arguments
  • The three-revolution frame. Cognitive revolution (~70,000 years ago — the arrival of language and the capacity for fiction), agricultural revolution (~12,000 years ago), scientific revolution (~500 years ago).
  • "Fictions" are the code for large-scale cooperation. Money, states, corporations, religions, human rights — all are what Harari calls "inter-subjective realities." Lions cooperate in groups of ten; humans cooperate in groups of hundreds of millions.
  • The agricultural revolution as "history's biggest fraud." Measured in individual quality of life, hunter-gatherers were healthier, worked fewer hours, ate better. Wheat domesticated us, not the other way around.
  • Empire as the natural terminus of cooperation. From Babylon to Rome to global capitalism — the through-line of human history is the steady expansion of the "imagined order."
Key passages
"We did not domesticate wheat. It domesticated us."
"How do you cause people to believe in an imagined order such as Christianity, democracy or capitalism? First, you never admit that the order is imagined."
Key concepts
  • Cognitive revolution
  • Inter-subjective reality (the "fictions")
  • The agricultural trap
  • Imagined order
  • The unification of humankind
In conversation with the others

This is the target of the issue. Homo Deus is its logical continuation. The Dawn of Everything can be read almost as a chapter-by-chapter rebuttal — especially of "the agricultural revolution" and "the origins of inequality." Boorstin does not engage Sapiens at all, but offers a stylistic alternative: write not about the "essence" of humanity but about its "exploration."

Who should read it / who can skip

Read if: this is your first encounter with the big-history genre, or you want to understand the reach of its influence. Skip if: you have already read Diamond's Guns, Germs, and Steel or Pinker's Better Angels, much of the early material overlaps. The "cognitive revolution" chapter is the boldest and thinnest in the book — read it with reservations.

Limits and controversies

Academic objections to Sapiens tend not to be about Harari's "views" but about his "use of evidence" — he writes conjectures as if they were facts, and contested chapters as if they were settled. He almost never cites the past twenty years of archaeology on early-human social complexity (Göbekli Tepe, Çatalhöyük, and others). Read it as "a persuasive narrative," not as "scientific conclusion."

Homo Deus: A Brief History of Tomorrow
Yuval Noah Harari
2016 · Harper · ~450 pages
The forward extension of Sapiens — if Harari writes the story, he gets to write the future too.
Core arguments
  • A new agenda after the three old ones. Famine, plague, and war have largely retreated; the new agenda is immortality, bliss, divinity.
  • Humanism is the de facto religion of modernity. Liberalism, socialism, and consumerism are variants — all share the premise that human feeling or choice is the ultimate source of meaning.
  • Dataism is displacing humanism. When the algorithm knows you better than you do, "I know best what I want" — the bedrock of humanism — collapses.
  • The "useless class." AI does not merely take jobs; it may produce large populations who are "neither economically valuable nor politically meaningful." Being not needed is worse than being exploited.
Key passages
"History began when humans invented gods, and will end when humans become gods."
"In the early twenty-first century the train of progress is again pulling out of the station — and this will probably be the last train ever to leave the station called Homo sapiens."
Key concepts
  • Dataism
  • The useless class
  • Algorithmic authority
  • The decoupling of intelligence and consciousness
In conversation with the others

Inherits Sapiens's narrative frame and projects it forward. Note the difference, though: Sapiens is contested on "how accurately it describes the past"; Homo Deus is contested on "how soundly it infers the future" — and the latter rests on much thinner ground. The "decoupling of intelligence and consciousness" looks prescient in the LLM era; the "useless class" looks overconfident. Graeber and Wengrow, writing in 2021, pointed and laughed at exactly this kind of teleological future-casting.

Who should read it / who can skip

Read if: you care about AI and society, or do long-horizon strategy. Skip if: the first third is largely a recap of Sapiens; if you've already read it, jump straight to Part Two.

Limits and controversies

Many predictions are delivered in an "I have seen through it all" register, but most rest on the 2016 technological landscape; from 2025 a number of them already look stale, too pessimistic, or too optimistic. Harari himself has walked back several in subsequent interviews. The best way to read it is "watch his framing of questions," not "trust his specific conclusions."

The Dawn of Everything: A New History of Humanity
David Graeber & David Wengrow
2021 · Farrar, Straus and Giroux · ~700 pages
Not a rebuttal of one book but of an entire generation of big history. If you read only one book on the other side, this is it.
Core arguments
  • "Prehistoric humans were simple" is an 18th–19th-century prejudice, not an archaeological conclusion. From Göbekli Tepe to Cahokia, mounting evidence shows ancient societies were startlingly complex — and often switched, seasonally, between very different political forms.
  • Inequality is not the inevitable outcome of agriculture. Agricultural societies that remained egalitarian are well attested; so are hunter-gatherer societies with steep hierarchies. The smooth "agriculture → settlement → inequality" arc in Sapiens or Diamond is crude.
  • "Political imagination" is the real through-line of human history. Humans are not driven by environment; they are driven by the collective question "what kind of society do we want to be?" — and historically that question was asked again and again, not once.
  • The Enlightenment concepts of liberty and equality drew in part on Indigenous critique — especially the Wendat thinker Kandiaronk. This is one of the book's most counter-intuitive and most disputed claims.
Key passages
"The ultimate question of human history is not our equal access to material resources, much though these matter, but our equal capacity to contribute to decisions about how to live together."
"If something did go terribly wrong in human history… it began to go wrong precisely when people started losing that freedom to imagine and enact other social orders."
Key concepts
  • Three primordial freedoms (to move, to disobey, to reshape society)
  • Seasonal political duality (the same group switching between two social forms across the year)
  • The Indigenous critique (its influence on European Enlightenment thought)
  • Schismogenesis (societies forming themselves by contrast with their neighbors)
In conversation with the others

A chapter-by-chapter dismantling of Sapiens (even if the book is restrained about naming names). Harari says agriculture was a "trap"; they say the "trap" metaphor presupposes a single road that never existed. Harari says "fictions" drive cooperation; they say political imagination is far richer than "collective hallucination." With Boorstin there is a distant echo — both care about "how we form our knowledge of the world," only Boorstin's frame is science and Graeber/Wengrow's is social form.

Who should read it / who can skip

Read if: Sapiens or Diamond's Guns, Germs, and Steel left you suspecting it was all "a bit too smooth"; or you work on organization design, community building, or want to understand "why our modern choices are not the only choices." Skip if: at 700 pages, with frequent digressions, this is a commitment. Time-pressed readers can start with the Introduction + Chapter 2 (on the Enlightenment) + Chapter 4 (on equality) + Conclusion.

Limits and controversies

Graeber died two weeks before publication, and the book has the air of a posthumous work — the structure is occasionally loose, the argument chain not always as crisp as the targets it criticizes. Working archaeologists hold reservations about specific readings of evidence (e.g., on the gender egalitarianism of Çatalhöyük). The "Indigenous critique influenced the Enlightenment" chapter has been intensely debated, and parts rest on inference. The reverse trap to avoid: don't accept this book as the new truth simply because it counters Harari — it too is a narrative, just another one.

The Discoverers
Daniel J. Boorstin
1983 · Random House · ~760 pages
Not "what humans are," but "how humans came to know." A fourth way of writing big history.
Core arguments
  • "The illusion of knowledge" is the greatest obstacle to discovery — far more dangerous than ignorance is the conviction that you already know. This is the book's keynote.
  • History organized by "how we came to know," not by dynasty or geography. The four volumes — time, the earth, nature, society — each trace how humans gradually extracted themselves from one or another illusion.
  • The pivots are usually tools, not geniuses. Clocks, maps, printing, the telescope, the microscope — the technology that made observations repeatable and testable is what created modern science.
  • The anonymous discoverers. Many decisive breakthroughs came from obscure craftsmen, merchants, and missionaries — not the "great scientists" of textbook fame.
Key passages
"The greatest obstacle to discovery is not ignorance — it is the illusion of knowledge."
"The great discoverers… were free to admit, again and again, that they did not know."
Key concepts
  • The illusion of knowledge
  • Tools as cognitive enablers
  • The anonymous discoverers
  • The four-volume structure: Time, Earth, Nature, Society
In conversation with the others

This is the fourth way of writing the issue — bypassing the arguments about "human nature" and "social form" altogether, and going directly at epistemology. The contrast with Harari is sharp: Harari writes "what we thought we knew turns out to be fiction"; Boorstin writes "we slowly traded fiction for testable truth." Both risk over-simplification, but in opposite directions.

Who should read it / who can skip

Read if: you work in science or technology, want to see "how knowledge itself is built," or find Harari's grand narratives a bit too slick. Skip if: the 1983 publication date shows in the slim treatment of non-Western scientific traditions (China, India, the Islamic world) — supplement with Joseph Needham or George Saliba. At 760 pages, read selectively by volume.

Limits and controversies

Boorstin's vantage is openly Western-centric and Whiggish (reading "rightness" backward from modern success). A 1983 book can hardly engage the sociology of science (Latour, Shapin) that complicated the idea of "objective truth" in the decades that followed. But as a narrative archetype — history written as epistemology rather than essence — it still works.

Reading order

Path A · Thesis → antithesis → alternative (the recommended path)

Sapiens → Homo Deus → The Dawn of Everything → The Discoverers. Take in the full Harari narrative (one book on the past + one on the future), then read Graeber and Wengrow's systematic rebuttal — you'll feel the distinct discomfort of being shaken awake — and then use Boorstin to see a completely different mode of writing, both a buffer and a deepening.

Path B · Time-limited (just two books)

Sapiens + The Dawn of Everything. The two poles of the debate. One mainstream, one its sharpest opponent. Once you've read both, decide whether to extend to Homo Deus (the future projection) and Boorstin (the epistemological alternative).

Path C · Reverse entry (recommended for readers who have already read Sapiens)

The Dawn of Everything → re-read Sapiens → The Discoverers → Homo Deus. Let Graeber and Wengrow strip the "silky" texture out of Sapiens first; when you return to Sapiens, your critical filter is on by default — that "second pass" is more rewarding than the first.

Questions to ask yourself after reading

  1. Which of Harari's claims do I accept simply because they "sound right" rather than because they are evidenced? Which can I directly refute using Graeber and Wengrow?
  2. "Our modern social form is a historical inevitability" — am I quietly using that assumption too? If it isn't inevitable, which present-day choices could have been otherwise?
  3. What does "the illusion of knowledge" look like in my own field? Have I been mistaking "knowing about" for "understanding"?
  4. Take the same set of facts and tell the story once using "fictions + mass cooperation" and once using "political imagination + social experimentation." How far apart are the conclusions? Which is closer to the truth?
  5. When I make long-horizon judgments — investing, career, organizational strategy — am I using a Harari-style teleology or a Graeber-style open-endedness? Which is the right tool for the problem I actually face?

Further reading

  • Guns, Germs, and Steel, Jared Diamond (1997) — the other lighthouse of the big-history genre. Graeber and Wengrow also single it out as a target; read it as the contrast case.
  • The Better Angels of Our Nature, Steven Pinker (2011) — published the same year as Sapiens; the optimistic narrative of declining violence. Also named by Graeber and Wengrow.
  • 1491, Charles Mann (2005) — a popular treatment of pre-Columbian American complexity; one of the bodies of evidence underlying The Dawn of Everything.
  • The Origins of Political Order, Francis Fukuyama (2011) — another big-history attempt, organized around institutions; tighter than Harari, far less widely read.
  • The Cambridge History of China — if Western "big history" feels too abstract, return to a Chinese-language scale and read a single dynasty at 70x detail as a calibration.
