Existentialism & the Absurd

The world does not answer "why," and we cannot stop asking. Four books, four ways of handling that crack — hold it open, leap over it, hand it to others, or live on top of it.

2026 · Book Recommendations · Issue 61

The Theme

The absurd is not the sulky claim that life is meaningless; it is a structure. We demand meaning, the world does not answer, and the absurd is the crack between the two that will not close. Camus's The Myth of Sisyphus works by refusing to seal the crack: suicide removes the one asking, faith fills it in, and both quietly change the question at the moment of answering it. Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling takes precisely the road Camus rejects — Abraham first performs an infinite resignation, then by virtue of the absurd takes Isaac back whole, at the cost of never being able to explain himself to anyone. Beauvoir's The Ethics of Ambiguity turns the monologue plural: a freedom that is not thrown toward the freedom of others spins in place. Shi Tiesheng's The Temple of Earth and I is that crack on the ground in Chinese — he swaps "why live" for "how to live," and what carries him is not an answer but a process.

Book by Book

The Myth of Sisyphus
Le Mythe de Sisyphe · Albert Camus · 1942
Published the same year as The Stranger · English: Justin O'Brien translation · about 150 pages
It opens by asking whether to kill yourself and closes by saying we must imagine Sisyphus happy. The hundred and fifty pages in between are Camus refusing every cheap way out, one at a time.
The Core Insight

Camus does not ask whether life has meaning. He asks whether, having found no ready-made meaning, you go on — which is why the book's first line makes suicide the one serious philosophical problem. It forces an abstract proposition into something only action can answer: you got out of bed today, and that is already a position.

The absurd is defined relationally, and this is the easiest thing in the book to misread. It is not in us and not in the world; it arises from the confrontation between the two. We are built to demand unity, clarity, reasons; the world simply runs on in silence. Remove either side and the absurd vanishes — it is a structure that requires both ends present at once.

From this follows his double refusal. Bodily suicide removes the one asking, conceding that the standoff is unbearable. And "philosophical suicide" — he names Kierkegaard, Shestov, Jaspers — uses a single leap to refill the silent world with meaning, abolishing the standoff just as surely. Both quietly change the question at the point of answering it.

What remains is to sustain it, which opens into three things. Revolt is confirming daily that the crack is still there and seeking no truce. Freedom comes from the collapse of ultimate purposes, which drops the full weight of choice back onto this moment. Passion replaces living better with living more — exhausting experience rather than refining it. These are not consolation prizes; they are the only things the premise of the absurd actually yields.

Then Sisyphus arrives: he pushes the rock up, the rock rolls back down, forever. Camus points the camera at the walk back down the mountain — the stretch where he is most conscious and sees his whole situation most clearly, and precisely where he stands higher than his rock. A fate is wretched only while it is judged wretched; shouldered with open eyes, the punishment changes character.

Key Passages
There is but one truly serious philosophical problem, and that is suicide. Judging whether life is or is not worth living amounts to answering the fundamental question of philosophy.
— The Myth of Sisyphus, opening of "Absurdity and Suicide"
The absurd is born of this confrontation between the human need and the unreasonable silence of the world.
— The Myth of Sisyphus, "The Absurd Walls"
The struggle itself toward the heights is enough to fill a man's heart. One must imagine Sisyphus happy.
— The Myth of Sisyphus, closing lines of the final essay
Where It Falls Short

The argument is literary, driven by images and momentum rather than rigorous inference. When he pins the label "philosophical suicide" on Kierkegaard and the others, his citation is rough and he barely engages their actual reasons. And how revolt, freedom and passion are supposed to follow from the absurd is never really derived — only declared.

Applying It

Use it on the long commitment that keeps making you ask "does this even mean anything". Camus's mechanism: do not answer that question, audit which method you are using to quietly abolish it. This week, write down the thing you are working on and list the three ultimate reasons you have invented for it (change the industry, financial independence, model something for your child), then cross them out one by one. If you would still open that file tomorrow morning with all three gone, it never rested on reasons. Then change the review question from "is it worth it" to "did I push today": the cure for the absurd is not finding a reason, it is decoupling the action from the reason.

Fear and Trembling
Frygt og Bæven · Søren Kierkegaard (published under the pseudonym Johannes de silentio) · 1843
Subtitled "A Dialectical Lyric" · several English translations · about 150 pages
A Dane fresh from breaking off his engagement uses Abraham's near-sacrifice of his son to ask a question that still has no safe answer: can a choice be right and yet impossible to explain?
The Core Insight

The pseudonym is itself the argument. He signs as Johannes de silentio — John the Silent — because the book is about something that cannot be said. Kierkegaard had just unilaterally broken his engagement to Regine Olsen and could explain to no one why the decision was right. Abraham is the extreme version he found for his own situation.

The comparison is the engine of the book. The tragic hero Agamemnon sacrifices his daughter for the expedition: brutal, but the whole city understands him, because he traded a lower ethical purpose for a higher one and never left the ethical. Abraham did not — God's command serves no communal interest, so he cannot defend himself in any universal language. He must therefore stay silent: the moment he speaks, he is either a murderer or a madman.

Hence the "teleological suspension of the ethical": above the particular and the universal there is a further relation, in which the individual stands directly before the absolute. It is the most dangerous sentence in the book, and he circles it endlessly without ever supplying a safe boundary — he knows it is indistinguishable in form from the self-justification of a fanatic.

The double movement is the technical core (see the figure). The knight of infinite resignation gives up what he loves, converts the love into an eternal form, and finds peace inside the pain — already an enormous achievement, and one that can be explained. The knight of faith takes one further step: having completed the resignation, he believes by virtue of the absurd that he will receive Isaac back in this life. He did not skip the giving up; he gave up and took the whole thing back.

Why must this step go "by virtue of the absurd"? Because no rational accounting supports it — Isaac has already been handed over. Kierkegaard's image is the dancer: leaping is not hard, the hard part is landing without a wobble, so that the landing is already walking. Which is why the knight of faith looks, from outside, exactly like a tax collector. All the drama happens where no one can see it.

Three movements · only the third is faith
Key Passages
He believed by virtue of the absurd.
— Fear and Trembling, "Eulogy on Abraham"
Is there a teleological suspension of the ethical?
— Fear and Trembling, title of Problema I
Infinite resignation is the last stage before faith, so that anyone who has not made this movement does not have faith.
— Fear and Trembling, Problema I
Where It Falls Short

The structure is deliberately circuitous: three "Problemata" build on each other and refuse to conclude, lyric alternates with dialectic, and a first reading struggles to find the thread. The more substantial trouble is that the teleological suspension admits no test — he concedes the knight of faith is invisible from outside, which also makes him indistinguishable from self-deception or fanaticism.

Applying It

Use it on the major, irreversible bet you cannot argue for to anyone — a pivot, an exit, committing a whole stretch of time to something nobody rates. Kierkegaard's mechanism is not encouragement to leap; it is a prior test: have you performed the infinite resignation? This week, write the "it already failed" post-mortem for the thing you are hesitating over — three years on, it came to nothing; what did you lose, and what do the days actually look like. Rewrite it until you can read it calmly, and only then decide. If you cannot get there, what you want is a guarantee. This one step separates conviction from wishful thinking: most people skip the resignation and go straight to the leap, which is not faith but a gamble.

The Ethics of Ambiguity
Pour une morale de l'ambiguïté · Simone de Beauvoir · 1947
English translation by Bernard Frechtman · about 160 pages · effectively the ethics that Being and Nothingness promised and never delivered
Sartre said we are condemned to be free, then never wrote the ethics to match. Beauvoir did, and she entered at the hardest point: if everyone is absolutely free, what stops me from treating you as a tool?
The Core Insight

She starts from human ambiguity: we are consciousness, able to project, a subject — and also flesh, prone to illness, available at any moment to be treated as a thing. This is not a contradiction awaiting resolution; it is the basic construction of a person. Every philosophy that tries to abolish the ambiguity, purifying us into spirit or into matter, ends up abolishing morality itself, because morality only has a place while both ends hold.

The key consequence is that freedom has two layers. Ontological freedom — you cannot avoid choosing — belongs to everyone and cannot be escaped, but it constitutes no value by itself. Real freedom is taking that passive freedom up actively and throwing it toward concrete ends. Which is how she can say what Sartre could not: some people's freedom has been crushed by their situation into idling, and that is oppression, not insufficient effort.

Her dissection of the postures that evade freedom is the best part of the book. The serious man treats values as ready-made things outside himself — the organization, tradition, his superiors — so that he need only obey and never bear anything. She calls this the most dangerous type, because his moral conviction is enormous and he is the readiest participant in atrocity. Beside him stand the nihilist (denying all value to avoid carrying any) and the adventurer (enjoying the thrill of acting without asking whom the act lands on).

Her definition of oppression is unusually sharp: oppression is freezing a person's transcendence into immanence — confining them to repeating the motions of staying alive, unable to project any future. Two years later that definition became the skeleton of The Second Sex, and it explains why her ethics is necessarily political.

The final step is the book's landing point: for my freedom to hold, someone else's freedom has to catch it. What I make survives me only if others freely take it up and carry it on. So willing oneself free and willing others free are not two acts but one — not a moral exhortation, but a structural necessity.

Key Passages
To will oneself moral and to will oneself free are one and the same decision.
— The Ethics of Ambiguity, Part II, "Personal Freedom and Others"
To will oneself free is also to will others free.
— The Ethics of Ambiguity, Part III, "Freedom and Liberation"
Where It Falls Short

She later said publicly that of all her books this is the one that most irritates her, finding it too abstract and still circling inside Sartre's vocabulary. The argument is dense and thin on examples, and the Part III material on violence and means carries an unmistakable period stamp (postwar left-wing France), leaving a few conclusions murky to a reader today.

Applying It

Bring it down to managing people and collaborating. Her test: does your arrangement give the other person more room to project, or only more actions to execute? This week, take something you are currently delegating and check whether the deliverable reads "make this work" or "do these seven steps" — the latter is freezing someone's transcendence into immanence. Then hold up the serious man as a mirror to yourself: the last time you said "that's just the process" or "it's what leadership wants," did it conveniently spare you your own judgment? This matters more in AI-assisted work, not less: treating the model as a tool is correct, treating people as interchangeable execution units is a different thing entirely, and organizations slide from one into the other without noticing.

The Temple of Earth and I
Wo yu Ditan · Shi Tiesheng · first published 1991 in Shanghai Literature · long-form essay
Collected in the essay volume of the same name · read alongside Fragments Written Between Illnesses (2002) · roughly 20,000 characters
The first three argue about meaning in a study. This one was thought through from a wheelchair, in a derelict imperial park, over fifteen years.
The Core Insight

Circumstance precedes thought. At twenty-one both his legs were paralyzed; he spent much of the rest of his life on dialysis and described himself as a professional patient who wrote on the side. He wheeled himself into the Temple of Earth not for a stroll but because in those years there was nowhere else to go. That fixes the character of the whole piece: here the absurd is not a proposition but a weight reloaded onto the body every morning.

The first section peels the question to the bottom. He thought hard about whether to die, and what he finally worked out was unexpected: death needs no hurrying, because it is certain to come. Once that is granted, suicide loses its urgency, and what remains is the harder and much longer question of how to live. The pivot of the whole essay sits there — and he says plainly that this question is not one any single moment can settle.

The park carries the weight of the argument. A four-hundred-year-old imperial altar left to decay, its glazed tiles eroded, weeds everywhere, bees and ants going about their business as usual. Nature offers no consolation; it simply does not stop — and it is that persistence, aimed at no one, that slowly draws him out of "why is the world doing this to me." This is another way of handling Camus's crack: neither filled in nor fought, but placed inside a longer rhythm.

The second section turns the camera off himself, and it is the heaviest part of the essay. His mother saw him out the door every day, followed him into the park in secret, and never let him see her; absorbed in his own pain at the time, he understood only afterward that a son's misfortune is always doubled in his mother. By the time his fiction was published and won prizes, she was gone. That section keeps the essay from being a story of personal redemption — he admits his own awakening was completed standing on another person's silence.

The conclusion is not "I figured it out." What he offers is the process itself: the gap between desire and capacity never closes, and "the sun, at every moment, is both a setting sun and a rising one." Meaning is not at the end; it is in the stretch you are still pushing through — nearly the same sentence as Sisyphus, except that Shi never reaches for the high note of revolt. What he offers is lower and more durable: keep going.

Key Passages
That a person has been born is no longer a debatable question but simply a fact handed to him by God… so death is not a thing to be hurried; death is a festival certain to arrive.
— The Temple of Earth and I, section 1
A son's misfortune is always doubled in his mother.
— The Temple of Earth and I, section 2
But the sun — at every moment it is both a setting sun and a rising one.
— The Temple of Earth and I, closing section
Where It Falls Short

It is an essay, not an argument: the conclusions arrive through image and rhythm, and a reader who does not take to that will not be caught by it — a few passages also edge toward self-moved sentiment. More importantly, the experience is intensely private, and generalizing "keep going" into a universal prescription slides easily into making light of other people's suffering.

Applying It

This one belongs on the body and the family — the two things abstract discussion most easily routes around. Shi's mechanism is placing an uncontrollable situation inside a longer rhythm, and a rhythm needs a concrete container. This week, pick one fixed place and slot — the same route, the same bench, the same twenty minutes, no phone, no purpose — and go every day for a week. The point is not relaxation; it is stepping back from "my problem" into "the things that are not about me are still moving." The second action comes from section two: write down one thing a family member did for you that you did not see at the time, then ask them this week how it felt. What hurts in that section is being too late, and being too late is not inevitable.

Questions Worth Asking

  1. The thing that keeps making you ask "what is the point of this" — if you never got an ultimate reason for it, would you still do it tomorrow?
    A framework

    Camus's test decouples the action from the reason, and there are three answers. First: no — it has been running on an external narrative all along, so cut your losses early. Second: yes, but only after telling yourself a story first — the story is a painkiller; keep it, just do not build on it. Most long commitments live here, which is nothing to be ashamed of; the danger is mistaking the painkiller for a conclusion, because when reality punctures the story people abandon the action along with it. Third: yes, and no story needed — that is what he means by living more.

  2. Your most recent decision that you could not justify: was it by virtue of the absurd, or just an unwillingness to face the cost?
    A framework

    Kierkegaard's test has one step: have you made the infinite resignation? The knight of faith hands Isaac over completely before there is any question of getting him back; a leap that skips the resignation is a gamble. One tell: people who have genuinely made the resignation are usually calm and in no hurry to convince anyone. Needing everybody to endorse your decision usually means you have not yet accepted the worst version of it yourself.

  3. The last arrangement you made — for a colleague, a child, yourself — did it give room to project, or only actions to execute?
    A framework

    Beauvoir's test is transcendence versus immanence, and you check three places: who set the goal, who bears the cost of failure, and whether the other person can propose something you did not think of. If all three are yours, that is immanence. Same with a child: a packed schedule versus one unscheduled afternoon differs not in workload but in whether there is any room left for them to project. Turn it on yourself too — if your entire day is built from other people's agendas, you are being frozen as well, only what freezes you looks a lot like a perfectly legitimate sense of duty.
