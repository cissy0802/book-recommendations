Four Styles of Writing

"Writing clearly" has at least four ways to teach it: a rulebook, a craft, a psychology, and a modern correction grounded in cognitive science.

2026 · Bidaily Books · Issue 4

Why these four together

Writing is not one thing. Strunk & White teach you to cut words, use the active voice, write short sentences — a 100-page rulebook that has stood for sixty years. Zinsser unfolds those rules into a complete craft of nonfiction: how to write about people, places, science, memory. Lamott barely talks about rules at all; she talks about why you can't sit down — fear, envy, perfectionism, shitty first drafts. Pinker arrives with twenty-first-century linguistics and cognitive science to tell you why several of Strunk & White's rules are in fact folklore, and to propose a more defensible standard — "classic style."

Read together, these four books make a single point. Every book on writing is solving the same problem — what is happening inside the reader's head — and they enter that problem through four entirely different doors.

The four books in detail

The Elements of Style
William Strunk Jr. & E. B. White
1959 (Strunk's 1918 in-house edition + White's 1959 expansion) / 1999 4th ed. · Allyn & Bacon · ~100 pages
The "Bible" of English-language style — thin enough for an evening, contested enough for six decades.
Core arguments
  • Omit needless words. The book's most famous line. Short is not the goal; every word has to do work.
  • Use the active voice, concrete nouns, strong verbs. Not "there is a … which …," but "X did Y."
  • Sentences need rhythm. Vary sentence length; let paragraphs have a logical spine.
  • Style is not ornament — it is honesty. E. B. White writes in the closing "An Approach to Style": style exposes the person; it cannot hide.
Key passages
"Vigorous writing is concise. A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences."
"Omit needless words."
The three words most often remembered from the book.
Key concepts
  • Omit needless words
  • Use the active voice
  • Definite, specific, concrete language
  • "An Approach to Style" — White's closing essay, the most literary chapter
In conversation with the others

The grandfather of Zinsser — almost every craft instruction in On Writing Well can be traced to a rule here. The target of Pinker — his whole book respectfully overturns several of these rules (passive voice, "hopefully," split infinitives). Hardly intersects with Lamott at all: Strunk cares about the words on the page; Lamott cares about the person before the page.

Who should read it / who can skip

Read if: you write emails, reports, or documents in English — two evenings will do. Skip if: you are a native English writer with ten years of practice; 80% is already internalized. For Chinese-language writers: read it as a training in "thinking about a sentence's responsibility," not as a list to mechanically transfer (Chinese does not need "avoid the passive voice").

Limits and controversies

Linguists — most famously Geoffrey Pullum, in "50 Years of Stupid Grammar Advice" — have pointed out that several of the book's "errors" are perfectly idiomatic English, and that Strunk & White's own examples often break their own rules. The best part of the book is not the rules but White's closing chapter — a small essay on "style as the person."

On Writing Well: The Classic Guide to Writing Nonfiction
William Zinsser
1976 first edition / 2006 30th anniversary · HarperCollins · ~320 pages
Unfolds Strunk & White's rules into a usable craft of nonfiction. If you read only one "how to write" book, read this.
Core arguments
  • The secret of writing is "stripping." Strip away cliché, bureaucratese, piled adjectives, and pretension — what remains is clarity.
  • Nonfiction has actual genres. People, places, memoir, science, business, sports, the arts — each has its own grammar. The second half of the book goes through them one by one.
  • Voice matters more than rules. "You are writing for yourself first" — not a license for narcissism, but an instruction to be honest.
  • Rewriting is the writing. Good writing is not written; it is rewritten. The only reason a first draft deserves to exist is to give you something to fix.
Key passages
"The secret of good writing is to strip every sentence to its cleanest components."
"Writing is hard work. A clear sentence is no accident. Very few sentences come out right the first time, or even the third time."
Key concepts
  • Clutter (every word that isn't doing work)
  • Unity of voice, pronoun, and tense
  • The transaction (writing transmits the writer's self to the reader)
  • Rewriting as the essence of writing
In conversation with the others

The operating manual for Strunk & White — turning "omit needless words" into ten chapters of specific situations. Complementary to Pinker: Zinsser teaches "how to do it," Pinker explains "why it works." Read together, your understanding leaps from intuition to principle. A gentle contrast with Lamott: Zinsser also writes about the psychology of writing, but his subject is "how to be more professional"; Lamott's is "how to fall apart more honestly."

Who should read it / who can skip

Read if: you write product documentation, technical posts, industry reports, or a personal newsletter. The second half divides by genre and can be read selectively. Skip if: you write fiction — the book barely touches it (that's Lamott's territory).

Limits and controversies

The models lean toward 1970s–90s American journalism and feel slightly dated. Some of the later chapters on "business writing" or "science writing" have specifics that have aged (its treatment of email is very 1990s). But the first half — Simplicity, Clutter, Style, Words, Usage — does not age.

Bird by Bird: Some Instructions on Writing and Life
Anne Lamott
1994 · Pantheon · ~240 pages
Not "how to write" — "how to sit down." Sanctuary for everyone who cannot get started.
Core arguments
  • "Shitty first drafts" are the only way through. The book's most-quoted chapter. Accept that the first draft will be garbage — it is the only way to begin.
  • "Bird by bird." The title comes from her father's advice to her brother, panicking over a bird-identification report due the next day: "Just take it bird by bird, buddy." Any large task can only be written in small pieces.
  • Short assignments — the "one-inch picture frame." Only write what fits in a one-inch picture frame in front of you right now. Don't think about the book; think about this paragraph, this character, this morning.
  • Envy, perfectionism, and the wish to be published are the real enemies of writing. Lamott spends more pages on these emotional obstacles than all the "how to write" books combined.
Key passages
"Perfectionism is the voice of the oppressor, the enemy of the people. It will keep you cramped and insane your whole life."
"Almost all good writing begins with terrible first efforts. You need to start somewhere."
Key concepts
  • Shitty first drafts (giving yourself permission to write badly)
  • Short assignments (don't think about the whole book)
  • The one-inch picture frame
  • KFKD radio (the internal station endlessly broadcasting self-deprecation and self-aggrandizement)
  • Index cards, character, plot, dialogue (the creative-craft chapters)
In conversation with the others

A prerequisite for the other three. Strunk & White, Zinsser, and Pinker all assume you can sit down and face the blank page — Lamott addresses that assumption directly. If you can't get the first draft out, no quantity of rules will save you. Shares Zinsser's faith that rewriting is the writing, but Lamott is more willing to admit how much it hurts.

Who should read it / who can skip

Read if: you are a writer paralyzed by procrastination and self-doubt; you've been trying to write your first book, first long essay, or first newsletter for months and are stuck; or you are a parent-writer (Lamott is a single mother; the book is full of real scenes of trying to write inside a life). Skip if: you only want to know how to make an email clearer — this is not a technical book.

Limits and controversies

Very American, very Californian, very Christian (Lamott writes openly as a Christian; some passages will feel like too much for non-religious readers). The chapter on "how to get published" has aged badly with the upheavals in publishing. The whole book is light on "technique" — but that is the point.

The Sense of Style: The Thinking Person's Guide to Writing in the 21st Century
Steven Pinker
2014 · Viking · ~360 pages
Uses linguistics and cognitive science to explain why certain writing is clear. The contemporary revision of Strunk & White.
Core arguments
  • The core of good writing is "classic style" — borrowed from Francis-Noël Thomas and Mark Turner. The metaphor of classic style: writer and reader are standing in the same room, and the writer points at something real and says "look there." Not persuasion, not performance — joint looking.
  • The curse of knowledge is the number-one cause of bad writing. Experts forget what non-experts don't know. Jargon, leaps, unspoken premises are all symptoms. Readers complain "I don't follow," while the writer thinks "I made it so simple."
  • Grammar is not folklore, it is a tool. Pinker devotes a whole chapter ("The Web, the Tree, and the String") to how sentence structure assembles in the reader's head: what the writer manages is not "rules" but the reader's working memory.
  • Several of Strunk & White's rules are wrong. Passive voice has functions (it avoids putting the agent in the wrong place); "hopefully" works as a sentence adverb; split infinitives have never been errors. Rules must be understood by function, not by tradition.
Key passages
"The curse of knowledge is the single best explanation I know of why good people write bad prose."
"Classic style is a pretense. The writer cannot literally make the reader see something with her own eyes, so she must use language to direct the reader's attention to something in the world."
Key concepts
  • Classic style (writer and reader looking at the world together)
  • The curse of knowledge
  • Cohesion vs. coherence (local connection vs. global organization)
  • Working-memory load on the reader
  • Arc of coherence (good prose is a tree, not a chain)
In conversation with the others

A respectful rebellion against Strunk & White — Pinker honors them openly while using linguistics to overturn or reinterpret several of their rules. Perfectly complementary to Zinsser: Zinsser gives you a checklist of "how," Pinker explains "why it works" — read together, your understanding upgrades from experience to principle. Almost orthogonal to Lamott: Lamott addresses "before writing"; Pinker addresses "what is happening in the brain while writing is happening."

Who should read it / who can skip

Read if: you work in technology (the chapter on the curse of knowledge is almost written for you), are an academic writer, or want to upgrade your "writing OS." Read if some of Strunk & White's rules have left you wondering "why on earth." Skip: Chapter 4 (syntax / tree structure) is technical for readers without a linguistics background — jump to Chapter 5 and come back.

Limits and controversies

Pinker speaks for the cognitive elite — his "classic style" is modeled on academic and high-end popular non-fiction, with weaker coverage of literary narrative, poetry, or feature journalism. The book runs long and example-dense; you may find yourself missing the slimness of Strunk & White. Pinker's positions on other matters (his "Enlightenment optimism") will color how some readers approach him — but this is a clean book about writing; don't let outside controversies decide it for you.

Reading order

Path A · From rules to understanding (recommended)

Strunk & White → Zinsser → Pinker → Lamott. Swallow the 100-page rulebook in an evening; let Zinsser expand the rules into working craft; let Pinker fill in the cognitive science of "why these rules work"; let Lamott address the real problem — "I understand it all, I just can't write." This order moves from the objective to the subjective, from outside to inside.

Path B · Time-limited (only two)

Zinsser + Lamott. One on the craft of getting words out, one on the psychology of sitting down. Between them they cover 90% of what actually goes wrong for someone who wants to start writing. You will absorb Strunk & White's rules indirectly through Zinsser; Pinker's theory can wait.

Path C · Stuck on the first draft

Lamott → Lamott → Lamott → one more time → then Zinsser. Solve the "I am too afraid to write" problem first. Lamott will not make you a better writer; she will move you from "not yet a writer" to "starting to be one." Technique can come later.

Path D · Already writing for more than five years

Pinker → re-read Strunk & White with a critical filter. Pinker will upgrade your metacognition: you will suddenly be able to explain why a paragraph isn't working, not just sense it. When you re-read Strunk & White, you'll agree with parts and disagree with others — which is the point.

Questions to ask yourself after reading

  1. In something you wrote recently, how many words are "doing work" and how many are "present but inert"? (The Zinsser strip test.)
  2. In your technical, product, or work documents, what does the curse of knowledge most often look like? At which sentence does the reader start losing you?
  3. The real reason you can't write — is it that you "don't know how" or that you "are afraid to"? (Lamott vs. Strunk.) If it is the latter, no rulebook will save you.
  4. The hidden standard that whispers "writing must look professional" — where did it come from? Is it helping you or holding you down?
  5. If you had to rewrite an old piece of yours next week, would you first apply Zinsser's strip method or first re-arrange sentence structure à la Pinker? The order of revision changes the texture you end up with.

Further reading

  • On Writing, Stephen King (2000) — half memoir, half writing book. Same lineage as Lamott but meaner and more professional. "The road to hell is paved with adverbs."
  • Draft No. 4, John McPhee (2017) — the legendary New Yorker feature writer on structure. If Zinsser is the introduction to nonfiction, McPhee is the master class — the chapter on structure alone is irreplaceable.
  • Several Short Sentences About Writing, Verlyn Klinkenborg (2012) — a fragment-by-fragment writing book on "how to write good sentences." A poetic cousin to Strunk & White.
  • Wenxin (《文心》), Xia Mianzun and Ye Shengtao (1934) — the most under-rated writing book in the Chinese tradition. Teaches middle-school students how to write by telling a story; the clarity of Republican-era vernacular is a benchmark that later Chinese textbooks never matched.
  • "Politics and the English Language", George Orwell (1946) — a 12-page essay on cliché, bureaucratese, and the corrosion of political language. Cited by Strunk & White, Zinsser, and Pinker alike.
