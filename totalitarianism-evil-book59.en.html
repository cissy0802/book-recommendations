Totalitarianism & Political Evil

Totalitarianism is not tyranny turned up louder; it is something new. These four books stand at different heights of the same machine: what conditions build it, what kind of person executes it, how it captures the finest minds, and who keeps it running day after day.

2026 · Book Recommendations · Issue 59

The Theme

Four books at four heights of one machine. Arendt's The Origins of Totalitarianism digs at the foundation: lonely masses, an ideology so self-consistent that no fact can refute it, and terror as an end rather than a means, each feeding the others. Eichmann in Jerusalem drops to the execution layer, where the man who handled the crime turns out to have no depth at all — she calls it thoughtlessness: never in dialogue with yourself, and therefore never able to stand in anyone else's place. Miłosz's The Captive Mind asks why the best-educated joined willingly; his answer is seduction plus Ketman — total outward compliance, complete inward dissent, and pride in the gap. Havel's The Power of the Powerless lands on the most ordinary layer of all: the real pillar is each person's small, convenient act of living within a lie — which also means that refusing to comply pulls the foundation out.

One machine · four heights

The Books in Detail

The Origins of Totalitarianism
Hannah Arendt · 1951
Harcourt, Brace · ~520 pages (revised 1958 and 1966)
Not a fiercer tyranny but an unprecedented form of rule — a tyrant wants your obedience; this wants to remake reality itself.
The core insight

Arendt wrote this book not to classify the twentieth century's catastrophe but to understand what had actually happened. Her first judgment already overturns common sense: totalitarianism is not despotism taken to an extreme. A despot wants obedience and silence — keep quiet, stay out of politics, and you can more or less live. Totalitarianism wants everything: not merely to suppress opposition but to take over your experience, your memory, your judgment. Its three parts (antisemitism, imperialism, totalitarianism) trace one thread: how modern Europe produced masses of people stripped of a place and available for total mobilization.

The first condition is loneliness. Totalitarian movements rest not on class or shared interest but on what she calls atomized, isolated individuals. She separates three states carefully: solitude is being in company with yourself — the place where thinking happens; isolation is losing the political capacity to act together with others; loneliness is the most complete — not even yourself for company. The third is the dangerous one, because it severs both lines by which a person calibrates reality: the world shared with others, and the conversation with oneself. A wholly lonely person has nothing left to lean on but logic.

The second condition plugs straight into that gap: ideology is the logic of an idea. Take a premise — history is class struggle, or nature is racial struggle — and deduce from it with perfect consistency, letting no fact back in. Its appeal lies precisely in that consistency: it explains everything, leaves nothing open, and gives the lonely a place to stand. Once the system closes, facts can no longer refute it: whatever fails to fit either has not yet emerged or was fabricated by the enemy.

The third is terror, and here her claim is counterintuitive: terror is not the means but the essence. A means stops once it has done its work; totalitarian terror accelerates after the opposition has been destroyed, because its function is not to remove enemies but to abolish the small predictable space between people — so that no one can make a promise to anyone. The three then feed one another: logic supplies terror with a script, terror drives people back into loneliness, and loneliness makes the next round of logic work better.

A triangle that feeds itself
Lines worth keeping
"The ideal subject of totalitarian rule is not the convinced Nazi or the convinced Communist, but people for whom the distinction between fact and fiction and the distinction between true and false no longer exist."
— The Origins of Totalitarianism, ch. 13, "Ideology and Terror"
"Totalitarian movements are mass organizations of atomized, isolated individuals."
— The Origins of Totalitarianism, part 3, ch. 10, "A Classless Society"
Where it falls short

The joints between the three parts are loose: how antisemitism and imperialism necessarily lead to totalitarianism reads more as narrative than causal chain. Placing Nazism and Stalinism under one form remains contested among historians, and the Soviet material was written before the archives opened. "Totalitarianism" was later conscripted wholesale by Cold War rhetoric — read her analysis apart from that political use.

Applying it

The transferable mechanism is "closed logic + immunity to facts = a self-feeding system" — which happens daily in far milder form inside technical organizations: an architectural judgment, a user persona, a growth narrative slowly hardens into a premise data may not touch, and counterexamples get filed under "bad sample" or "too early." Next week run a falsifiability check: take one core claim your team keeps quoting, write down "what observation would prove this wrong," then actually go look for it. If you cannot write that sentence, it is no longer a hypothesis but a creed. The second point is closer to home: the foundation is loneliness — the people most easily captured by any complete narrative are the ones who appear only in tickets and reviews, with no lateral connections. This week, book a one-on-one with the quietest person on your team that has nothing to do with delivery.

Eichmann in Jerusalem
A Report on the Banality of Evil · Hannah Arendt · 1963
Viking Press · ~310 pages (1965 revised edition adds a postscript)
She went to see a monster and found a hollow functionary — the evil can be vast while the man who does it has no depth at all.
The core insight

In 1961 Arendt covered the Jerusalem trial for The New Yorker, expecting a demon and finding a middle-aged bureaucrat with a poor vocabulary, a mouth full of clichés, and an eye on promotion. "The banality of evil" comes from that, and has been misread for sixty years since — she never meant that the evil is small. She meant the opposite: the consequences can be astronomical while the person producing them shows no matching depth. Evil need not be done by demons; that is what is genuinely disturbing.

The mechanism she names is thoughtlessness, and she insists it is not the same as stupidity (Eichmann was not dim; he was an able administrator). In her usage, thinking is a silent dialogue a person conducts with themselves; someone who never holds that dialogue has no way to stand in another person's position. The most striking detail in court was that Eichmann could barely finish a statement in words of his own — even describing his own life he reached for ready-made official phrases. The emptiness of the language is not a rhetorical problem; it means there was nobody home in that position.

The system supplied a further layer of insulation: she documents the Nazi language rules (Sprachregelung) — "final solution," "special treatment," "resettlement." These euphemisms were not there to fool outsiders but to let the participants never, in their own minds, kill anyone: they merely arranged transport and timetables. Responsibility is cut into fragments, and every fragment stays clean: each person can honestly say he only did his own part.

But Arendt does not let structure swallow responsibility. The hardest passage in the book is the judgment she writes for the court herself in the epilogue: whether or not he was a cog, he did take part in executing the removal of an entire people from the earth, and therefore no one can be expected to share the world with him. Explaining how a person became this does not excuse him — that is the balance point of the whole book, and the half most often skipped.

Lines worth keeping
"The trouble with Eichmann was precisely that so many were like him, and that the many were neither perverted nor sadistic, that they were, and still are, terribly and terrifyingly normal."
— Eichmann in Jerusalem, ch. 15, "Judgment, Appeal, and Execution"
"It was sheer thoughtlessness — something by no means identical with stupidity — that predisposed him to become one of the greatest criminals of that period."
— Eichmann in Jerusalem, postscript to the 1964 edition
Where it falls short

The crucial problem: she attended only part of the trial, and Bettina Stangneth's Eichmann Before Jerusalem (2011), drawing on the Sassen interviews from his years in Argentina, shows a deliberate and proud antisemitic ideologue — the courtroom banality was very likely a performance. As an empirical portrait of Eichmann himself, "the banality of evil" is probably wrong; as a description of most participants in a bureaucratic apparatus, it remains sharp. Her passages accusing the Jewish Councils of collaboration also assert far more than her material supported.

Applying it

The mechanism is "euphemism + division of labour → action severed from consequence → nobody is thinking." This is exactly the structure that AI and automated systems reproduce most easily: model, policy, threshold, rollout — each person owns one segment, and the final consequence is stated as a metric: "optimize retention," "clear out low-quality users," "tighten the risk threshold." Next week take one automated decision you signed off on and do two things. First, translate the jargon into plain speech — a sentence with a subject and someone on the receiving end: "this rule will cause a certain kind of user to lose a certain thing." Second, write down what you would see, and whether you could appeal, if you were the person the rule hits. If you cannot write the first sentence, the decision was never thought about, only processed. Worth hardening into a norm: every launch review carries a plain-language description of consequences.

The Captive Mind
Zniewolony umysł · Czesław Miłosz · 1953 (trans. Jane Zielonko)
First published by Instytut Literacki, Paris; English translation the same year · ~250 pages
What captures intellectuals is rarely a gun — it is a worldview that explains everything, plus the pleasure of standing on the right side of history.
The core insight

Miłosz (Nobel Prize in Literature, 1980) asks a different question: why did the best-educated people join willingly? He has standing to ask — after the war he served postwar Poland as a diplomat, defecting to Paris in 1951, and the book was written after the break. So it is not outside observation but a confession still warm to the touch: he had been one of the men he describes.

The first device is the Murti-Bing pill, borrowed from Witkiewicz's novel Insatiability: whoever swallows it becomes serene, because he has accepted an entire philosophy that explains the world. The temptation facing postwar Eastern European intellectuals, Miłosz says, was not the instrument of torture but this pill — atop the ruins, a doctrine claiming to have grasped the laws of history supplied three scarce goods at once: order, meaning, and "I stand on the right side of history." What is hard to resist is never the threat; it is a world that has been explained.

The second device is Ketman, taken from Gobineau's account of Persia: total outward conformity while keeping every disagreement intact inside — and taking pride in it. He catalogues several kinds: national, aesthetic, professional, ethical, metaphysical. What matters is that it pays: the disguise itself confers a sense of intellectual superiority, the feeling that one has not really surrendered but is merely acting; every fluent act of insincerity feels like a small victory.

And here falls his hardest stroke: play the part long enough and you grow into it, until you can no longer tell which self is real. Ketman is not a technique for preserving the self but a process that consumes it — in the end, even the most intimate people speak to each other in Party slogans. The second half of the book gives four portraits of writers (Alpha the Moralist, Beta the Disappointed Lover, Gamma the Slave of History, Delta the Troubadour), each a different slope down: moral fervour, nihilism, power, or simply the wish to keep writing.

Lines worth keeping
"It was only toward the middle of the twentieth century that the inhabitants of many European countries came, in general unpleasantly, to the realization that their fate could be influenced directly by intricate and abstruse books of philosophy."
— The Captive Mind, ch. 1, "The Pill of Murti-Bing," opening line
"After long acquaintance with his role, a man grows into it so closely that he can no longer differentiate his true self from the self he simulates."
— The Captive Mind, ch. 3, "Ketman"
Where it falls short

It is a literary essay, not an analytic work: no systematic evidence, winning instead on insight and portraiture, which will disappoint readers looking for argued mechanism. The four portraits shadow real contemporaries (Andrzejewski, Borowski and others) and are not necessarily fair to them; Miłosz had served the regime himself, so an element of self-defence runs through the book. Beware over-extension above all — it describes one decade and one stratum, and using it to indict all conformity dilutes what was specific about the totalitarian situation.

Applying it

The mechanism is "Ketman pays, so people don't stop — and the role eventually becomes the person." Its low-grade modern version is everywhere: you don't believe some narrative, yet you speak it fluently — OKR-speak, industry jargon, the investor line — while privately congratulating yourself that you are merely playing along. Miłosz's warning is exactly that this feeling of lucidity is itself the anaesthetic. Next week run a fifteen-minute Ketman audit: list the last three occasions on which you said something you do not believe, and tag each one — "lubricant" (harmless social smoothing) or "corrosion" (it has begun to shift your real judgment; you notice you now think that way in private too). For anything tagged corrosion, set a line: this is a thing I can decline to say, and I accept the cost. It matters most around children — they learn not the principles you state but how you speak under pressure.

The Power of the Powerless
Moc bezmocných · Václav Havel · 1978 (trans. Paul Wilson)
Czech samizdat; English translation in the 1985 collection of the same name · long essay, ~100 pages
What holds the system up is not belief but the small daily act of going along — which means that stopping pulls the foundation out.
The core insight

Havel wrote this underground in 1978, after Charter 77. What he faced was no longer Stalinist mass terror but what he calls the post-totalitarian system: nobody believes the ideology any more, yet the entire ritual runs on, sustained by general, weary, unspoken compliance. It is the harder opponent: no perpetrator to point at, only countless variations of "what could I do?"

He explains the whole mechanism with one example. The greengrocer puts "Workers of the world, unite!" in his shop window. He does not care about the workers of the world; the sign's real message is a different sentence: I am obedient, so please leave me alone. The subtlest turn comes next — if he were told to display "I am afraid and therefore unquestioningly obedient," he would feel shame at once. The function of the slogan is to dress obedience in something that looks selfless, so that he can hide from himself what he is doing. The system supplies not only pressure but a respectable way to describe it.

From which follows the essay's pivot: if the pillar is the daily compliance of countless people, then the line of conflict does not run between the system and the people but through each person — everyone is, in their own way, both victim and supporter. That cut removes the comfort of "them and us."

Where the line actually runs

The power of the powerless grows from exactly there: if the foundation is everyone's daily compliance, then ceasing to comply pulls that foundation out. The greengrocer takes the sign down; materially this is nothing, symbolically it is lethal — he has demonstrated in public that another way of living is possible. It also explains a long-puzzling phenomenon: why such regimes react to apparently trivial gestures with wildly disproportionate ferocity. They know better than anyone where their foundation lies. Hence Havel's insistence that "living within the truth" is not a political programme but an existential starting point; independent organizations are its result, not its precondition.

Lines worth keeping
"Individuals need not believe all these mystifications, but they must behave as though they did, or they must at least tolerate them in silence... For this reason, however, they must live within a lie."
— The Power of the Powerless, section III
"The line of conflict ran de facto through each person, for everyone in his or her own way was both a victim and a supporter of the system."
— The Power of the Powerless, section III
Where it falls short

"Post-totalitarian" is a precise historical category — the frozen, ritualized late communism; applied to periods of high terror or to contemporary democracies it loses focus immediately. The moral demand of "living within the truth" verges on heroism and offers little concrete guidance to people who must compromise to feed and protect a family; it can even become a reproach. Havel's later presidency exposed the gap between dissident ethics and actual governing.

Applying it

The mechanism is "the system runs on daily compliance → the smallest non-compliance carries outsized weight." It holds in any organization: what keeps a distorted picture of reality running is never one bad actor but dozens of instances of "fine, it reads better this way." Next week do one small concrete thing — pick a "sign" you have been hanging up out of habit and take it down once: don't write the progress phrasing you don't believe into the weekly report; or say your actual judgment in a meeting where everyone is nodding (even just "I have reservations, and here's why"); or stop forwarding a number you know is framed misleadingly. Then note the cost. You will most likely find the real cost is far below what you expected, while the value to the person beside you who also didn't dare speak is far above it — which is precisely the weight Havel meant.

Questions to sit with

  1. In the system you work inside, is there a premise that facts can no longer refute?
    A framework

    Use Arendt's test, not your gut: any claim for which you cannot state "what observation would prove this wrong" has already turned from hypothesis into creed. Check three places — your basic assumptions about users, your judgment on a technical direction, your assessment of your own organization's capability. Then look at what happens to counterexamples: last time data failed to fit, was it taken seriously or filed under "bad sample" and "too early"? Twice or more, and the system has closed. A closed system's mark is not that someone is lying — it is that no input can change the output.

  2. Which sentence you recently said, wrote, or nodded along to was one you do not believe?
    A framework

    Ask on two levels. Havel's level asks about function: what is the sentence really transmitting? If it translates to "I am obedient, please leave me alone," would you still say it — shame is an accurate detector here. Miłosz's level asks about cost: you think you are performing with your eyes open, but for how long now? Has there been a moment when you started thinking that way in private too? That is the role beginning to consume you. Handle the two separately: harmless social lubricant needs no crusade; the ones already shifting your real judgment deserve a line you are willing to pay to hold.

  3. That decision you signed off on — if its consequences were described in plain speech instead of jargon, could you still sign?
    A framework

    Replace every abstract noun in the description with a sentence that has a subject and someone on the receiving end: not "optimize the conversion funnel" but "who will see what, and lose what," then read it again from the position of the person affected. Two danger signals: you cannot state the consequence in plain speech (it was never thought about, only processed); or you can, but instinctively want to add "the process decided this" (responsibility has already been fragmented — and her conclusion was that explaining is not excusing).
