Issue 10 · Themed Reading List

Meaning & Suffering

When pain, loss, and death can't be sidestepped, where does meaning come from — a real redemption to be discovered, or a psychological construct we build to hold off the void?

2026 · Bidaily Books · Issue 10

Why these four

Suffering is not optional. Frankl in a concentration camp, Shi Tiesheng in a wheelchair, Yalom at the bedside of the dying, Solomon in a psychology lab — four people press, from four entrances, on the same question: when suffering can't be avoided, where does meaning come from?

Frankl says meaning is discovered, and that even suffering can carry it. Solomon spends thirty years of experiments arguing the reverse — that nearly everything we treasure as meaning is largely a defense against the terror of death. Yalom, working in the clinic, urges us not to look away but to "stare at the sun": living toward death makes us live more truly. Shi Tiesheng offers no method; over fifteen years in an old Beijing park he wrote suffering into something one can live alongside.

Put together, these four are not a "self-help list" but a real disagreement laid bare: is meaning redemption, or a necessary illusion? The disagreement is not just a seminar debate — it shapes how you face the hard thing you are in right now. If meaning is real, go and find it. If meaning is only a defense, seeing that clearly is already a kind of freedom. If both are half true, what you need is Shi Tiesheng's posture: to keep living, and writing, with the doubt intact.

The four books in detail

Man's Search for Meaning
Viktor E. Frankl
German first edition 1946 / English 1959 · Beacon Press · ~165 pp.
A manifesto on meaning written at the most extreme site of suffering — the source of logotherapy.
Core arguments
  • The deepest human drive is the "will to meaning" — not Freud's will to pleasure nor Adler's will to power. The break with Freud is also directional: psychoanalysis looks backward (childhood determines the present); logotherapy looks forward (a future meaning summons the present self to become).
  • Meaning can't be invented, only discovered, and it lives in three places — "creative values" (work, the giving to the world), "experiential values" (love, beauty, nature), and most distinctively "attitudinal values" (the stance taken toward unavoidable suffering). The third is logotherapy's signature contribution: when the situation cannot be changed, meaning can still be found in how I bear it.
  • Suffering that finds a meaning ceases to be mere suffering. Trudging through the freezing dark of the camp, Frankl held the image of his wife's face (not knowing she had already died elsewhere); in that instant, he writes, he understood for the first time that "love is the ultimate and the highest goal to which man can aspire," and "the salvation of man is through love and in love."
  • The last freedom: in the camp everything was stripped away, except the freedom to choose one's attitude in any given circumstance.
  • Tragic optimism: even facing the "tragic triad" of pain, guilt, and death, one can still say "yes" to life — not blind cheer, but choosing meaning in full knowledge of suffering.
Key quotes
"Everything can be taken from a man but one thing: the last of the human freedoms—to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances, to choose one's own way."
(Part One)
"Suffering ceases to be suffering at the moment it finds a meaning."
Frankl also borrows Nietzsche's line: "He who has a why to live for can bear with almost any how."
Tension with the others

A head-on clash with Solomon: Frankl believes meaning is a real, discoverable redemption; Solomon holds that meaning systems are, at bottom, defenses against the terror of death. The same "meaning" — one an exit, the other a defense mechanism. That is the question to carry through this issue.

Limits & controversy

Drawn from an extreme setting, the "freedom of attitude" is easily over-generalized into "if your mindset is right, any suffering is worth it" — close to romanticizing pain, which some survivors find offensive. Logotherapy's clinical evidence is also thinner than that of cognitive behavioral therapy.

Staring at the Sun: Overcoming the Terror of Death
Irvin D. Yalom
2008 · Jossey-Bass · ~320 pp.
An existential psychiatrist's guide to death-anxiety, written for ordinary readers.
Core arguments
  • Much anxiety is repressed death-terror in disguise. It shows up wearing other clothes: midlife crisis, runaway shopping or hoarding, an inability to be alone, anxious over-investment in one's children, compulsive checkups, free-floating emptiness. The presenting problems differ; the same thing is often ringing the bell underneath.
  • The idea of death can save us: grasping that life is finite awakens us to live more authentically — the book's central thesis.
  • Awakening experiences: serious illness, the death of a loved one, a child leaving home, retirement, major failure — these natural "death-adjacent" moments are the most effective catalysts of awakening, and Yalom's therapy works with them rather than around them.
  • Epicurus dissolves the fear: where death is, I am not; where I am, death is not. The nothingness after death mirrors the nothingness before birth — why fear it?
  • Rippling: our influence on others passes outward in widening circles beyond the body — a secular "immortality" and consolation. Healing comes not from ideas but from relationship and presence: we fear death most in isolation.
Key quote
"Though the physicality of death destroys us, the idea of death saves us."
The book's guiding thesis.
Tension with the others

Yalom and Solomon look at the same death yet prescribe opposites: Solomon describes how people repress and avoid death-terror (and so turn xenophobic and dogmatic); Yalom argues that only facing it frees us. Repression vs. confrontation is the issue's sharpest pair. With Frankl he is complementary but subtly different: Frankl's meaning is discovered, Yalom's is generated in engagement and relationship.

Limits & controversy

Heavy on cases, light on theory; closer to essay than argument. Epicurus-style reasoning is often not enough against real death-panic (emotion isn't talked out of itself by logic). Individual-leaning, with little on collective or social suffering.

The Worm at the Core: On the Role of Death in Life
Sheldon Solomon, Jeff Greenberg & Tom Pyszczynski
2015 · Random House · ~288 pp.
Turns the philosophical claim of Ernest Becker's The Denial of Death into thirty years of experimental social psychology.
Core arguments
  • Terror Management Theory (TMT): humans are the only animals who clearly know they will die. Becker described that condition as a "god who shits" — a self-conscious creature reaching toward the eternal (thought, art, love) while trapped in a body that will rot. That fissure is the root of the anxiety.
  • Two lines of defense hold it down: (1) cultural worldviews (religion, nation, value systems) that confer meaning and some form of "immortality" — literal immortality (afterlife, reincarnation) or symbolic immortality (works, children, the continuity of a people); (2) self-esteem — the conviction that one is a valuable member of that meaningful world. Self-esteem is not a luxury but a firewall against death-anxiety — which is why being dismissed or ignored hurts far beyond what the surface warrants: it strikes the root of the defense.
  • Mortality salience: once reminded of death, people defend their worldview harder, favor their own group more, grow more hostile to outsiders, and punish transgressors more severely. In one much-cited study, judges asked first to imagine their own death set bail several times higher in the same case.
  • An unsettling corollary: prejudice, fanaticism, consumerism, the worship of power, the swing toward more conservative and nationalist politics after a terrorist attack — all may be death-terror being quietly activated. Carrying this lens into current events, plenty of phenomena suddenly show their underside.
Key quote
"…the skull will grin in at the banquet."
William James, The Varieties of Religious Experience; the book takes its title, "the worm at the core," from his image of the awareness of death. Becker put it more bluntly: the fear of death "haunts the human animal like nothing else."
Tension with the others

This is the issue's counterpoint. Frankl treats meaning as redemption; Solomon treats it as a defense mechanism — the same phenomenon, opposite readings. It lends Yalom empirical backing (death-anxiety really is everywhere) yet parts ways with him: Yalom wants you to look, while TMT describes exactly how hard people work not to.

Limits & controversy

TMT experiments have also been caught up in psychology's "replication crisis," with some mortality-salience effects questioned. Its explanatory reach is so wide it is hard to falsify (anything can be traced back to fear of death), and the authors sometimes reduce complex social phenomena to death-terror a little too forcefully.

Me and the Temple of Earth · Notes Between Illnesses
Shi Tiesheng (史铁生)
Me and the Temple of Earth 1991 · Notes Between Illnesses 2002 · essays
Contemporary China's deepest writer of suffering: no method, only a posture for living alongside it.
Background

Paralyzed in both legs at 21, later on long-term dialysis for kidney failure, he died in 2010, describing himself as "a professional patient who writes on the side." The Temple of Earth is the old Beijing park where he sat and thought for years after his paralysis. Running alongside the park in Me and the Temple of Earth is a second thread — his mother, who bore his most desperate years in silence and searched the park for him, until only after her death did he read the love she never spoke. Suffering here is never one person's alone.

Core realizations
  • The park itself is the metaphor: a Ming-dynasty altar forgotten by its era, walls crumbling, weeds taking over — and still there, still alive. A paralyzed young man sat for fifteen years inside an old thing that had been abandoned and yet went on existing. From the park he learned: to be abandoned is not the same as to be over.
  • Put death back where it belongs: death is not a problem to solve but an appointment sure to be kept; once that is clear, the present becomes vivid instead.
  • Suffering can't be "figured out" once and for all, but it can be lived with, over and over. In fifteen years at the park he did not find answers; he learned to coexist with their absence.
  • Rediscovering everyday grace within illness: suffering is a step-by-step education in contentment.
  • The process is the meaning: we don't live in order to reach some meaning; living, getting through, is itself the meaning. His late "faith" does not point to any religious God — it is a process-shaped belief turned toward goodness and meaning: "where the heart turns toward the good, there a god is."
Key quotes
"Death is not something to rush toward; death is a festival that will inevitably arrive."
— Me and the Temple of Earth
"Only when you run a fever do you know how crisp the fever-free days are; only when you cough do you know how serene a throat that doesn't cough."
— Notes Between Illnesses
Tension with the others

He echoes Yalom's "living toward death" from the East: the man in that park is exactly the kind Yalom describes as "awakened" by the idea of death. Like Frankl he seeks meaning within suffering, but by the opposite road — Frankl is systematic (logotherapy), Shi is unsystematic; he distrusts "figuring it out" and would rather coexist, long-term, with the unresolved.

Limits & controversy

Essayistic and aphoristic, with no argumentative structure — readers who want a framework will find it "scattered." His later "faith" carries a personal mysticism that may not persuade secular readers, and his experience is so singular that it resists being generalized into a method.

Suggested reading order

Frankl → Yalom → Solomon → Shi Tiesheng

Begin with Frankl to set up the humanist claim that suffering can hold meaning. Use Yalom to land it on death — the hardest reality — and to offer the clinical posture of facing it. Then let Solomon's empirical counterpoint shake the first two hard: maybe the meaning you treasure is only a defense? Finally return to Shi Tiesheng: in a non-Western, unsystematic life, watch a man neither pretend meaning is a guaranteed redemption nor coldly dissect it into defense, but keep living and writing with his doubt intact. Thesis — development — antithesis, and Shi is the synthesis.

Questions to ask yourself after

  1. The pain I'm now avoiding (illness, loss, aging, failure) — if I ask, with Frankl, "what attitude does it ask of me," what is the answer?
  2. Is my stance toward death closer to Solomon's "working hard not to look" (busyness, consumption, identity holding it down), or Yalom's "staring at it"?
  3. The belief or identity I defend most fiercely — could it be, in part, shielding me from the fear of death?
  4. If, like Shi Tiesheng, I treated death as "a festival sure to arrive" rather than a problem to solve, would today's priorities change?
  5. My "ripples" — in whom have I already left an influence that will carry on?
