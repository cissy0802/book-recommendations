The Language of Poetry

Same words as everyone else uses — so what exactly does a poem do that everyday language cannot? Four poets, four wholly different answers.

2026 · Book Recommendations · No. 53

Introduction

Poetry uses fewer words than everyday speech to reach places everyday speech cannot — but how it reaches them, each poet answers oppositely. In the Duino Elegies, Rilke drives language toward the unsayable — existence, death, the angel — "transforming" the invisible inner world into sayable images. Neruda runs the other way, stooping to name onions, tomatoes, socks, proving that finding the right word for the humblest thing is itself an act of love. Whitman smashes meter, using the breath of long lines and the catalog to let a single "I" hold an entire clamorous democratic America. And Du Fu dances inside the strictest shackles of regulated verse, where constraint itself hammers out a precision no other means can reach.

The Four Books in Detail

Duino Elegies
Duino Elegies · Rainer Maria Rilke · begun 1912, finished 1922, published 1923
Recommended English: Stephen Mitchell or Leishman/Spender · ten elegies
Poetry's hardest task: to find un-warping words for the things that warp the instant you speak them — being, death, love, the angel.
The Core Insight

Rilke began the Duino Elegies in 1912 on a cliff at Duino Castle by the Adriatic. He is said to have heard, in the roaring wind, the line "Who, if I cried out, would hear me among the angels' hierarchies?" — then stalled for a full ten years, until in 1922, at Muzot in Switzerland, the whole cycle poured out in a few weeks. His subject is the hardest there is: a human being's smallness and solitude before the vastness of existence. His angel is not the Christian angel but a symbol of "a completed, terrible beauty" — so whole that a person simply cannot bear it. The opening line — "beauty is nothing but the beginning of terror, which we are still just able to endure" — names the tension of the whole book in a stroke: the highest beauty and the deepest terror are one and the same thing.

Rilke assigns poetry an almost impossible job, which he calls "transformation" (Verwandlung). The Ninth Elegy asks: why, in the end, are we here? The answer is astonishing: "Perhaps we are here in order to say: house, bridge, fountain, gate, jug, fruit tree, window." Our calling is to take the perishable, visible world and, through language, "say" it into the inner life, say it into the imperishable — to turn the outer into the inner, the visible into the invisible. The poet is the agent of that transformation: to find a word for the unsayable is to save the world from vanishing, once.

The ten elegies are called "elegies," yet they turn near their close toward an astonishing affirmation — "being here is glorious." Rilke's logic: precisely because everything perishes, precisely because we are "the most fleeting" of all, this moment of existence is beyond price. Mourning and praise are not two things but two faces of looking at the same thing. This lifts the Elegies beyond private feeling into a question about how a mortal being can be at home in mortality.

The strongest experience of reading the Elegies is of language pushed to the edge of breaking — sentences long and coiling, images nested in images, often not "understood" yet striking home. This is exactly what Rilke wants: for some truths, the clarity of everyday language is precisely what kills them; they can only be "hinted at" by language driven to its limit. He proves a power unique to poetry — not to make things clear, but to preserve what cannot be made clear, and keep it glowing.

"Who, if I cried out, would hear me among the angels' hierarchies?"

"For beauty is nothing but the beginning of terror, which we are still just able to endure."

Limits

Extremely hard to read, and hugely dependent on the translation — English versions differ sharply in tone, and a poor choice can make Rilke read as willfully obscure metaphysics. "Angel" and "transformation" carry a heavy private mysticism; readers who don't accept that personal mythology may feel he is peddling the unprovable. On a first reading, pair it with a reliable guide rather than forcing the original.

For BigCat

Rilke's "transformation" is a precise tool for your deep interest in consciousness and Buddhism. Those intuitions that surface in meditation, or when reasoning with AI reaches some edge — the "I know it but can't say it" ones — usually get waved past, or handed to the AI to summarize away. Try this week: catch one such unsayable moment and resist summarizing it; do what the Ninth Elegy does — find it a single concrete object (a door, a well, a stone) and force yourself to carry the invisible feeling on a visible thing. This is not a literary exercise but a technique for archiving fleeting inner experience; an image you've said aloud can be relit later, whereas an abstract label ("very profound," "very moving") is simply gone in two days.

Selected Neruda
Twenty Love Poems and a Song of Despair (1924) · Elemental Odes (1954)
Pablo Neruda (Chile, 1904–1973) · Nobel Prize 1971 · English: W. S. Merwin, and others
The opposite direction from Rilke — poetry need not gaze up at angels; stooping to name an onion or an old sock is the highest language too.
The Core Insight

Neruda was one of the most prolific, most "loud" poets of the twentieth century, with two faces across a lifetime. At twenty, his Twenty Love Poems wrote of love in scorching images of body and nature — the line "Tonight I can write the saddest lines" is memorized by every adolescent in the Spanish-speaking world. In middle age he turned to something wholly different, the Elemental Odes: solemn odes, one by one, to the humblest everyday things — onion, tomato, salt, socks, the dictionary.

The revolution of the Odes is to aim the register of epic at the most ordinary objects. He calls the onion a "luminous flask," and writes of it peeling off layer by layer like shedding clothes. This is no belittling joke — Neruda sincerely held that poetry's long habit of writing only of gods, heroes, and love is a kind of snobbery; true poetry should be able to condescend to find a word worthy of a tomato, a sock. To find an exact, tender word for an ordinary thing is itself an act of love and respect.

This line runs together with his politics (a Chilean communist and diplomat): language should not be the private property of an elite. The Odes first appeared in a newspaper, written for ordinary readers; he wanted the porter and the fisherman to recognize, in a poem, the very things they touch each day. This "democratization of poetry" descends directly from Whitman — Neruda openly named Whitman his spiritual father.

The craft Neruda teaches his reader is "the concrete." Sorrow is not stated in the abstract as "I am sad" but as: "Write, for example, 'The night is starry and the stars are blue and shiver in the distance.'" — emotion always landing on a touchable object. This "transitive" writing is an antidote to all hollow lyricism: not to "state" the feeling, but to present the thing that carries it.

Key Lines
"Tonight I can write the saddest lines."
— Twenty Love Poems and a Song of Despair, XX
"I like for you to be still: it is as though you were absent."
— Twenty Love Poems and a Song of Despair, XV
"Onion, luminous flask, your beauty formed petal by petal."
— Elemental Odes, "Ode to the Onion"
Limits

Extreme productivity also means extreme unevenness — Neruda simply wrote too much; the love poems can be maudlin, and the late political verse (such as lines dedicated to Stalin) reads as acutely embarrassing today. His loud, unstoppable flow is a different aesthetic from the compression of Rilke and Du Fu; readers who prize restraint may not stomach his flood of images. A good selection pays off far more than reading the complete works.

For BigCat

Neruda's "to name an ordinary thing is love" is a language game you can play directly with a child. Try this week: write an "elemental ode" together — no grand words, just the least noticeable thing in the house (an old sneaker, an umbrella, the breakfast egg), and give it three to five lines of praise. What this trains is the most precious skill of all: turning "seeing" into "seeing clearly," and then into "saying." For a school-age child this beats memorizing ornate phrases — it pulls language back from performance to attention to the real world, and along the way grows a child's affection for the ordinary things nearby.

Leaves of Grass
Leaves of Grass · Walt Whitman · first edition 1855, revised lifelong
Walt Whitman (USA, 1819–1892) · the "deathbed edition" is the final revision
Whitman shattered centuries of English meter and invented a free verse like breath, like speech, like the sea — big enough to hold a whole clamorous democratic America.
The Core Insight

When Leaves of Grass first appeared in 1855, English poetry had been ruled by meter and rhyme for centuries. Whitman all but single-handedly invented modern free verse — unrhymed, un-metered, driven forward by the parallelism, repetition, and inner rhythm of long lines, reading like a sermon, like waves, like a man letting his voice out full. The scale of that formal liberation is hard to overstate: nearly all later modern poetry, from Neruda to Ginsberg, are his descendants.

Whitman's signature technique is the "catalog" — a long list of concrete things, trades, and kinds of people set side by side as equals. He will spend dozens of lines listing, without judgment, the blacksmith, the boatman, the president, the slave. The deep meaning of this device: by exhausting all things and granting them equal standing within the poem, language "enacts" democracy itself — no one nobler than another, all the voices sounding equally in one poem.

"Song of Myself" opens, "I celebrate myself, and sing myself," and this "I" is no small self but a vast subject able to contain everyone. The famous line — "Do I contradict myself? / Very well then I contradict myself" — has Whitman deliberately renouncing logical consistency, because real life and a real people are full of contradiction. To hold the whole of America, the "I" must be large enough to contradict itself.

In the Victorian age he openly celebrated the body, sex, sweat, placing flesh and soul on equally sacred footing — a scandal at the time (Leaves of Grass was once boycotted as "obscene"). But this is precisely his creed: democracy cannot be only a political slogan; it must come down to honoring every body, every kind of labor, every ordinary person as sacred. His poem is a spiritual constitution for the American experiment.

Key Lines
"I celebrate myself, and sing myself."
— "Song of Myself," opening
"Do I contradict myself? / Very well then I contradict myself, / (I am large, I contain multitudes.)"
— "Song of Myself," section 51
"I sound my barbaric yawp over the roofs of the world."
— "Song of Myself," section 52
Limits

The unstoppable flow cuts both ways — he kept adding to Leaves of Grass all his life, and some passages are long-winded, self-repeating, near-preachy. His optimism and grand "American" narrative need discounting against their historical context (his actual stance on race was more complicated than the poems' ideal). Translation loses a great deal, too: once the rhythm of free verse leaves the colloquial cadence of the English original, it easily goes flat.

For BigCat

Whitman's "I contain multitudes, and need not be consistent" is a counterintuitive permission for the "AI super-individual" you're pursuing. The super-individual's anxiety often comes from forcing yourself to converge into one logically consistent role (technologist? creator? parent?). Whitman's answer: you need not unify — only contain. Try this week: take your identities scattered across fields — distributed-systems engineer, AI explorer, writer, parent, student of Buddhism — and, like a Whitman catalog, write them out side by side, unranked, not forced to serve one another. Looking at the list itself brings a kind of integration: they need not converge into a single KPI; their coexistence is your vastness.

Selected Du Fu
Yeh Chia-ying's readings of Du Fu · also Xiao Difei's annotated selection
Du Fu (712–770) · read by Yeh Chia-ying, a master of classical Chinese poetry
The opposite of Whitman's meter-smashing: Du Fu locks himself into the strictest shackles of regulated verse, yet hammers out, within those inches, the highest precision and weight in Chinese poetry.
The Core Insight

Du Fu, honored as the "Sage of Poetry," raised regulated verse (eight lines, strict tonal patterns, the middle two couplets required to be antithetical) to a height no one has matched. Regulated verse is among the most heavily constrained forms in Chinese — count, tone, and parallelism are all hard rules. Far from feeling shackled, Du Fu dances his most precise dance inside those chains: "The View from a Height" is called "the finest regulated verse of all time," every one of its eight lines antithetical yet never stiff.

Set within this list, Du Fu and Whitman form the sharpest contrast: Whitman wins freedom by breaking form, Du Fu wins power by embracing it. Strict meter works like an exoskeleton, forcing the poet to weigh the sound, sense, and position of every character — "so bent on the perfect line that I won't rest till my words astonish." Constraint is not the enemy of creation; it is exactly what forces out a precision and density no looser means can reach. "Boundless the falling leaves come rustling down; / endless the great river rolls its waves along" — in fourteen characters, space, time, sound, and momentum are all present; remove one word and it is wounded.

But Du Fu's greatness exceeds technique. Tossed about all his life, living through the An Lushan rebellion, he welds his own wandering, cold, and hunger — the loss of a child — to the collapse of an entire age. "The state is shattered; mountains and rivers remain" — a nation's ruin and nature's indifference, all in eight characters. This fusion of personal suffering with the fate of the nation, the quality later called "somber and halting," gives his poems the weight of epic; posterity calls them "poet-history."

What moves most is the radius of his compassion. His own thatched cottage torn open by the autumn wind, sleepless in the rain, what he thinks of is: "Oh, for a mansion of a thousand thousand rooms, to shelter all the world's cold scholars, every face aglow!" — from one man's cold reasoning out to all the cold of the world. This benevolence is never an abstract slogan; it always lands in a wholly concrete situation (the leaking roof, the road of flight, the conscript's weeping). Du Fu proves that the strictest meter and the widest mercy can live in one poem.

Key Lines
"Boundless the falling leaves come rustling down; endless the great river rolls its waves along."
— "The View from a Height" (Deng Gao)
"The state is shattered, yet mountains and rivers remain; spring in the city, and grass and trees grow deep."
— "Spring View" (Chun Wang)
"Oh, for a mansion of a thousand thousand rooms, to shelter all the world's cold scholars, every face aglow!"
— "My Cottage Unroofed by Autumn Winds"
Limits

Du Fu's threshold lies in allusion and meter — without a good annotated edition, a modern reader can hardly grasp the subtlety of tonal parallelism, and easily reads him only as a "care-for-nation" label of ideas, missing the forging of the language itself. His "somber" ground note is heavy; read too much at once and it oppresses — pair him with Li Bai and others for air. Yeh Chia-ying's readings excel at rendering the beauty of meter into language a modern reader can grasp — a good crutch to start with.

For BigCat

Du Fu's "constraint forces out precision" is the most practical — and most counterintuitive — lesson here for a technologist. We default to "more freedom is better"; Du Fu proves that deliberately adding constraint forces out quality. Try two places this week: one, when writing or prompting an AI, impose hard limits on purpose — "in three sentences, none over fifteen words, no adjectives"; the harsher the constraint, the more it forces out precise expression (this is exactly the principle of regulated verse). Two, in engineering design, swap the default assumption of "unlimited resources" for a Du-Fu-style hard frame (fixed budget, fixed interface, fixed byte count) and watch how the constraint forces out a more elegant solution. Freedom is not always creativity's friend; the right shackles are.

Questions to Ask Yourself

  1. The last time you had an intuition or experience you "knew but couldn't say," did you let it slip away — or, like Rilke, find it a concrete word or image?
    A frame

    The test isn't "how nicely you said it" but "can this image relight the original feeling later." If yes, you've successfully archived a fleeting inner experience; if no, you probably just slapped on an abstract label ("moving," "profound"), and next time it really is gone. The art of transformation is to give the invisible a visible anchor.

  2. When you want to express a strong emotion or judgment, do you reach for abstract big words ("important," "beautiful," "painful") — or for an object concrete enough to touch?
    A frame

    A self-check: pull up a recent passage where you wrote in feeling or anger, and count the ratio of abstract adjectives to concrete nouns and actions. The power of Neruda and Du Fu is almost entirely in the latter. More abstract words: you're "stating" the emotion; more concrete objects: you're "presenting" it — and only the latter truly reaches a reader.

  3. Facing the same creative or engineering problem, do you trust the Whitman move — "smash all limits for freedom" — or the Du Fu move — "embrace the strictest constraint for precision"? When should you use which?
    A frame

    Both are right; it turns on the phase. In the exploratory phase, when you need to break the existing frame, Whitman's liberation finds paths no one has walked; in the converging phase, when you need to polish something to its edge, only Du Fu's constraint forces out precision. The danger is using them backwards: still diverging endlessly when it's time to converge (nothing ever ships), or binding your own hands when it's time to explore (only local optima). First ask yourself — "Am I finding the mountain, or climbing it?"
