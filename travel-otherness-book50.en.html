Travel & the Other

We think travel means going to see the world — yet more often we only see the expectations we brought with us. When you reach a strange place, do you truly meet the Other, or only your own projection? These four books are four ways of seeing the Other.

2026 · Book Picks · Issue 50

Introduction

Four travelers, four postures toward the Other. Kapuściński pushes his whole body into Africa — living in the worst districts, crossing the continent by truck while sick with malaria — trying to dissolve the shell of the "European observer" entirely. Chatwin walks to the southernmost tip of South America chasing a scrap of "brontosaurus skin" from his grandmother's cabinet, confessing that travel is often a myth we tell ourselves. Theroux makes the train itself the protagonist, recording with an unromantic, almost caustic honesty the truth of strangers briefly colliding in a railway carriage. Xuanzang travels for seventeen years to fetch scripture and records more than a hundred lands, laying down a thousand-year yardstick for "how to record the Other" by keeping the seen and the merely heard strictly apart.

Four Ways of Seeing the Other

Four travelers on two tensions

The Four Books in Detail

The Shadow of the Sun
Heban · Ryszard Kapuściński · 1998 (Polish), 2001 (English)
Knopf · ~336 pages
A Polish reporter who refused the correspondents' comfortable hotels, returning to Africa again and again over forty years — living in the slums, burning with fever, climbing onto broken-down trucks. He believed you must give up your own body before you have the right to write the Other.
The Core Insight

Kapuściński was Poland's correspondent in Africa, crisscrossing the continent from the late 1950s into the 1990s. His very first sentence refuses the word "Africa": it is a planet made of countless languages, peoples, climates, and histories — and the moment a European says "Africa," he has already begun to simplify and distort. His method is therefore anti-aerial: he never writes an abstract continent, only specific people and specific encounters.

First mechanism: lived experience as method. He deliberately avoided the hotels where foreign correspondents clustered, moving instead into the most dangerous district of Lagos; he nearly died of cerebral malaria and crossed the desert on trucks with no air conditioning that broke down at any moment. This choice to "lower himself to the same conditions as the locals" was meant to strip away the observer's privileged distance — not to watch Africa through a car window, but to sweat inside its dust, disease, and fear.

Second mechanism: from the smallest concrete detail, grasping a whole different logic of survival. He writes how the sun, while you wait for a truck to be fixed, bakes a person down into a shadow; why "waiting" here is not wasted time but life itself; why the word "tribe" is closer to reality than the word "nation." His gift is to let a trivial scene suddenly give you the skeleton of another worldview.

Third mechanism (and the central paradox of this issue): the truth of literature, or the truth of journalism? Kapuściński is a master of "literary reportage," and late in life this drew controversy — critics (such as his biographer Domosławski) note that he would exaggerate, merge, even invent certain scenes for narrative power. This forces the sharpest question in travel writing: to make a distant Other "become real" for the reader, may a writer — should a writer — deploy the craft of fiction? He walked that line his whole life.

Fourth mechanism: handing the viewpoint back to the one being watched. He always looks at history from the side of the colonized — the wounds colonialism left, the chaos after independence, the Cold War powers jockeying across the continent. He lets European readers, for the first time, examine themselves from the angle of "how Africans see the white man." This is the hardest and most precious step in meeting the Other: not merely I see you, but seeing myself anew through your eyes.

Key Quote
"In reality, except as a geographical appellation, Africa does not exist."
— The Shadow of the Sun, opening of the Introduction
Limitations

The techniques of literary reportage leave its accuracy in doubt — some of his most moving scenes do not survive fact-checking, and later biography exposed the exaggeration and invention within them. And he remained, after all, a Polish white man's gaze; writing African women, or regions where he never stayed long, he still slid into generalization and romance. Read it as "the subjective testimony of an honest outsider," not as an anthropological record.

Application for BigCat

Kapuściński's "give up your body, strip away the observer's privilege" maps straight onto research in the AI age. When you use AI for industry or user research, the comfortable move is to sit in the "luxury hotel" — let the model aggregate secondhand reports and generate personas, seeing the Other through a layer of data. Next week, try the reverse: for a group you are analyzing with AI (a user type, an unfamiliar market), go spend a full hour with one real person from it, observing without presupposition. You will find that what the AI's "average persona" erased is exactly the concrete, illogical detail that makes the group real. AI handles scale; you handle firsthand presence — neither can be skipped.

In Patagonia
In Patagonia · Bruce Chatwin · 1977
Jonathan Cape · ~240 pages
A scrap of "brontosaurus skin" in his grandmother's cabinet sent him to the far southern tip of the Americas — Chatwin proves that travel is often not about reaching a place, but about completing a myth you've told yourself since childhood.
The Core Insight

The book opens with a piece of skin in a glass cabinet in his grandmother's dining room — the young Chatwin was told it was the skin of a "brontosaurus" (in fact a giant ground sloth, a mylodon), sent back from a Patagonian cave by a distant cousin. That scrap became a lifelong obsession. Years later he dropped his editorial job in London, left only the note "I've gone to Patagonia," and set off to find where the skin came from. The whole journey's motive is a private, almost fairy-tale notion.

First mechanism: a landscape assembled from fragments. The book is built from more than ninety short chapters, like shards picked up off the ground — a snatch of family history, an exile's testimony, the legend of Butch Cassidy hiding out here, the tracks of Darwin. He gives you no continuous itinerary, but a "world's end" woven from stories, legends, and scraps of history. Patagonia, under his pen, is not a place; it is an imagined realm layered from the stories of countless exiles, fugitives, and utopian dreamers.

Second mechanism: the traveler as "story collector." Wherever Chatwin goes, he is dredging up other people's stories — Welsh settlers, German émigrés, anarchists. His interest in the Other is not immersion (as with Kapuściński) but collection: turning strangers into legend, turning geography into narrative. It is another way of being with the Other, and it carries its own danger.

Third mechanism (the opposite pole of this issue): a serene blurring of fact and fiction. The book became a classic on publication and has borne a doubt ever since — he heavily cut, rearranged, even invented dialogue and scenes, and some locals written into the book complained openly that "it wasn't like that at all." Chatwin blurred the line between travelogue and novel; what he wanted was not factual accuracy but narrative truth. If Kapuściński closes on reality through the body, Chatwin serenely concedes: travel writing is always a re-creation, and the Other has always been reshaped by the one telling the tale.

Fourth mechanism: nomadism as conviction. The obsession running through his whole life was that question — why do human beings wander restlessly instead of sitting still? He believed human nature is nomadic and that settlement is what violates it. Patagonia — desolate, shifting, unable to hold anyone — was the perfect stage for that conviction.

Key Quote
"In my grandmother's dining-room there was a glass-fronted cabinet and in the cabinet a piece of skin. It was a small piece only, but thick and leathery, with strands of coarse, reddish hair."
— In Patagonia, Chapter 1 (the book's opening)
Limitations

The blurring of fact and fiction cuts both ways — the Patagonia you read is as beautiful as it is edited and invented, and locals written into the book complained publicly. The fragmentary structure also lets the whole slip through your fingers, like leafing through someone else's scrapbook. Readers who want the real Patagonia will be disappointed; what it offers is a poetics of travel, not the reality of a place.

Application for BigCat

What Chatwin exposes is the psychology of "the quest" — we think we are chasing an external goal, when in fact we are completing an inner myth. That is a warning for major decisions (an investment, a pivot, choosing a path for a child). Next week, before a big decision you are wrestling with, ask yourself the Chatwin question: what I am really chasing — is it the value of the thing itself, or a story about "who I am" that I told myself long ago? Write the motive out in two columns: "objective reasons" and "personal myth." You will often find the larger driver is the latter — and seeing that is not to deny the myth (myth gives power), but to stop it from masquerading as rational judgment.

The Great Railway Bazaar
The Great Railway Bazaar · Paul Theroux · 1975
Houghton Mifflin · ~342 pages
Not to reach any destination, but to ride every train from London to Tokyo and back again — Theroux makes "the journey itself" the protagonist, recording with an unromantic, almost caustic honesty what travel is actually like.
The Core Insight

Theroux's radical move is a reversal: the traditional travelogue writes "the place"; he writes "the way there." In 1973 he took the train from London, through Eastern Europe, Turkey, Iran, India, Southeast Asia, and Japan, then returned via the Trans-Siberian — four months spent almost entirely in carriages. He effectively declares: the essence of travel is not the sights but the movement itself — the carriage, that temporary, forced-intimate little society.

First mechanism: the train as a theatre for watching humanity. The famous opening line, quoted by countless travel writers since, names his love of trains: a self-sufficient pleasure of sleeping berths, scenery flowing past the window, and an endless supply of strangers. He turns the platform and the carriage into the finest stage for observing human nature.

Second mechanism: unromantic honesty. Theroux's defining trait is that he never prettifies. He writes the boredom, the filth, the swindles, the dysentery, the insufferable fellow passengers; he sketches every companion without mercy, including their vanity, lies, and absurdity. This honesty corrects the genre's habitual flattery: real travel is mostly waiting, discomfort, and tedium, lit only occasionally by wonder.

Third mechanism: the Other is not in the scenery but in the next seat. The "Other" he meets is usually not an exotic figure before a monument, but the passenger beside him — a down-and-out writer, a boastful salesman, an eccentric old Englishwoman. In the sealed space of forced coexistence, people drop one mask and put on another. Theroux's insight: your understanding of a strange culture often comes not from grand landmarks, but from the few hours you endure with one specific person in a swaying carriage.

Fourth mechanism: travel with a novelist's eye. Published in 1975, the book made Theroux famous overnight and all but reshaped modern travel literature. Its lasting value is that it models an attitude: don't judge whether the scenery is beautiful, only record how people expose themselves in particular situations. It still reads well half a century on, because human nature hasn't changed — only the train has become the budget flight.

Key Quote
"Ever since childhood, when I lived within earshot of the Boston and Maine, I have seldom heard a train go by and not wished I was on it."
— The Great Railway Bazaar, opening line of Chapter 1
Limitations

The caustic edge cuts both ways — his portraits of people along the route (especially in Asia) often carry a sense of superiority and prejudice, and some passages read today with the flavor of a colonial gaze. He cares more about his own clever observations, at times flattening a foreign country into a backdrop for his complaints. The book goes deep into no local life; it skims across, rather than entering.

Application for BigCat

Theroux's "the journey is the destination, unromantic honesty" is a tonic for the efficiency-driven technologist. We habitually compress everything into "arrival" — go straight to the conclusion, skip the process, use AI to take the shortcut. Next week, pick one thing you habitually want to shortcut (learning a new field, running some research, finishing a project with your child) and deliberately take the "slow train": don't rush to the conclusion; make the process itself the object of observation and record, noting the concrete details and surprises the shortcut would have skipped. Theroux proves it: the most valuable observations are hidden precisely in the stretch of road you wanted to cut out.

Great Tang Records on the Western Regions
Da Tang Xiyu Ji · Xuanzang (compiled by Bianji) · 646 CE
12 fascicles · records 138 lands
A monk who traveled west for seventeen years to fetch the true scriptures — fifty thousand li, one hundred and thirty-eight lands seen or heard of — dictating the record on his return. This is the Eastern high-water mark for the very act of "recording the Other."
The Core Insight

In 627 CE Xuanzang left the frontier without permission to seek the Dharma in the west, passing through the Western Regions and Central Asia to India, studying for years at Nālandā monastery, and returning to Chang'an in 645 with more than six hundred Buddhist texts. The next year, by imperial command and with his disciple Bianji as scribe, he organized all he had seen and heard into the twelve-fascicle Great Tang Records on the Western Regions, documenting the geography, customs, products, politics, and religion of 138 lands. It is at once a pilgrimage record and an encyclopedia of seventh-century Central and South Asia.

First mechanism: the seen and the heard kept strictly apart. The most remarkable thing about his method is the rigorous distinction he draws between places he personally reached and places he only heard about — scholars can tell, from his wording, which are firsthand and which are hearsay. This self-awareness about the source of information was an astonishing scientific attitude for the seventh century: he knew secondhand knowledge cannot be equated with the eyewitnessed, and this is exactly the core discipline of "how to honestly record the Other" — clearer-eyed than many a modern travelogue.

Second mechanism: giving the Other its right name. Regarding India, he set down that famous correction: the old renderings "Shendu" and "Xiandou" were inaccurate, and following the correct local pronunciation it should be called "Yindu" (India). He refused to keep passing on his predecessors' garbled transliterations, insisting on returning to the actual sound to name an Other — getting someone's name right first is a small but profound act of respecting the Other's own subjectivity.

Third mechanism: an outsider becomes the key to the Other rebuilding its own history. India long lacked a continuous tradition of historiography; many ancient place-names, Buddhist holy sites, and dynastic successions could be recovered only thanks to the precise record of Xuanzang, an outsider. In the nineteenth century archaeologists used it to follow the map and unearthed the ruins of Nālandā and confirmed the sites of the Buddha's life. A traveler's faithful record ultimately became the evidence by which that land reconstructed its own history — the highest value "recording the Other" can reach.

Fourth mechanism (and the one most worth remembering): every "objectivity" carries a frame. Unlike the other three, Xuanzang's journey had one overriding purpose — to seek the Dharma. The eye with which he saw the world was always the eye of a Buddhist monk: he records holy sites, stupas, and the sizes of monastic communities in exhaustive detail, while matters unrelated to the faith are comparatively brief. It reminds us: even the most rigorous record is filtered through the recorder's purpose and belief — and Xuanzang is no exception.

Key Quote
"As for the name 'Tianzhu' (India), opinions have long been in confused dispute. The old renderings said 'Shendu,' or else 'Xiandou'; now, following the correct pronunciation, it should be called 'Yindu' (India)."
— Great Tang Records on the Western Regions, Fascicle 2, General Account of India
Limitations

It is, in the end, a monk's catalog-like record — heavy on religion and geography, light on personal feeling. You will not read Xuanzang's fear, loneliness, or shifting moods on the road (those live in his disciples' Biography of the Tripiṭaka Master of the Great Ci'en Monastery). The prose is terse and archaic, the entries densely packed — more a local gazetteer than a travelogue, and ordinary readers will find it dry. And its "objectivity" is always filtered through a Buddhist frame: holy sites in detail, the secular world in brief.

Application for BigCat

Xuanzang's discipline of "the seen and the heard kept apart" is a literacy most worth rescuing in the AI age. When you use AI, the model kneads firsthand fact, secondhand relay, and pure fabrication into one and the same fluent, confident tone and serves it up — all information looks equal in provenance. Next week, set yourself a Xuanzang rule: for every key conclusion produced through AI, force a source-tier label on each — "verified myself," "has a reliable source," "AI claims it but unverified." Just as Xuanzang separated the firsthand from the hearsay, keep "seen" and "heard" forever apart in your notes. This single move blocks most of the misjudgments AI hallucination would otherwise cause.

Questions to Ask Yourself After Reading

  1. On this trip (or some "faring far" in your life), did you truly meet the strange Other, or only the expectations and projections you carried with you before you left?
    A perspective

    One test: after you got back, how many concrete facts could you name that ran contrary to what you expected before leaving? Not a single one — and what you saw was probably just yourself (the Chatwin myth). Many details that unsettled you and forced you to revise your prior understanding — and you truly met the Other (the Kapuściński mode of firsthand experience). The depth of a journey isn't measured by how far you went, but by how much your presuppositions were corrected by reality.

  2. Facing a group, culture, or market you don't understand, are you giving up your body and going firsthand like Kapuściński, or sitting in the "luxury hotel" reading secondhand briefs?
    A perspective

    The modern default (especially with AI) is "hotel mode" — knowing the Other through aggregated data and other people's conclusions, efficient but one layer removed. Ask yourself: of what I know about this subject, how much did I see and hear firsthand, and how much is relayed? When the ratio tilts heavily toward the relayed, however confident the judgment, it is a castle in the air. Even one firsthand sample can overturn the whole impression you got from the reports.

  3. When you record or relay anything (writing a report, vouching for an AI's conclusion, passing something on to others), do you keep "seen" and "heard" apart the way Xuanzang did?
    A perspective

    Take apart a recent important judgment and label each piece of evidence: verified myself / reliable source / hearsay or AI fabrication. If most of what supports the conclusion is the latter two, you are building on hearsay. Xuanzang's thousand-year wisdom in one line: first sort out what you know from what you've merely heard, then decide how far to trust it.
