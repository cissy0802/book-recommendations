Issue 11 · Themed Reading List

The Innovation Curve

How do new things go from nothing to something, from the fringe to the mainstream? Why do incumbents get killed by unglamorous nobodies, while startups religiously chanting "lean iteration" are mocked as incapable of building anything truly large?

2026 · Bidaily Books · Issue 11

Why These Four

"Innovation" has been used so much it has lost its shape. Each of these four books grabs one point on the innovation curve that others missed. Moore saw the break in the middle of the curve — not that the product isn't good enough, but that early customers and mainstream customers are simply two different kinds of people. Christensen saw something counterintuitive: good companies don't fall because they get worse, they fall because they are too rational. Ries saw that almost all "progress" in a startup is an illusion — you can hit every milestone and still be building something nobody wants. Thiel turns around and rejects the incrementalism the first three share, with an uncomfortable claim: competition itself is a destroyer of value. Read the four not to memorize four labels, but to see the one thing each author actually saw — clearly enough to restate it and apply it to the work in your own hands.

The Four in Detail

Crossing the Chasm
Geoffrey A. Moore · 1991 (3rd ed. 2014)
HarperBusiness · ~272 pages
Success in the early market does not carry over to the mainstream automatically — because the people buying from you have switched from one kind of person to a completely different kind.
What Makes This Book Distinctive

Everyone has seen the smooth bell curve of adoption (innovators → early adopters → early majority → …). Moore's originality was to notice the curve is actually discontinuous between "early adopters" and "early majority" — a chasm cracks open there. The issue isn't the technology; it's that these two groups are psychologically different buyers.

Early adopters are visionaries: they buy a vision, they're willing to be first, they even take pride in "nobody else uses this yet." The early majority are pragmatists: they buy only what peers have already validated and can be deployed safely; they refuse to be the guinea pig. There is no transition zone between these two buying logics — the visionary's enthusiastic endorsement is, to a pragmatist, precisely a danger signal.

Hidden here is a fatal circular trap: pragmatists trust only peer references, but if everyone waits for someone else to go first, there's never a first peer, and the product stalls in the chasm — your early customers are tapped out, and not a single mainstream customer moves. Moore's solution is counterintuitive because it demands you deliberately shrink the market to something tiny: pick one excruciatingly painful niche whose members talk to each other (not "sell to enterprises" but "sell to the regulatory-filing department at pharma companies"), and pour everything in to dominate it absolutely. Once that small circle has a few success stories passing by word of mouth, you have manufactured with your own hands the social proof pragmatists demand, and you topple the adjacent niches in turn — his "bowling-pin" play. In other words, crossing the chasm isn't about a better product; it's about cutting the market small enough that you can become "the one everybody uses" inside it.

Key Quote
"The point of greatest peril in the development of a high-tech market lies in the transition from an early market dominated by a few visionary customers to a mainstream market dominated by a large block of customers who are predominantly pragmatists in orientation."
— Crossing the Chasm, Chapter 1
Limits

The cases are mostly 1990s enterprise software; the details have aged. In an era of two-sided platforms, SaaS, and AI apps, a single beachhead may not be enough. The theory is strong on qualitative pattern, weak on numbers — "where exactly is the chasm" tends to be clear only in hindsight, hard to locate in advance.

The Innovator's Dilemma
Clayton M. Christensen · 1997
Harvard Business Review Press · ~286 pages
Giants get killed by new technologies not because they grow dumb or lazy — precisely because they do every line in the "good management" textbook right.
What Makes This Book Distinctive

The usual explanation for the decline of big companies is "arrogance, rigidity, missed opportunities." Christensen's subversion is to argue a colder conclusion: the very rational, good habits that made a company successful are what systematically drive it toward death. This isn't a competence problem you can fix by trying harder — try harder at "good management" and you die more reliably.

The mechanism lives in resource allocation. When a disruptive new technology first appears, it has worse performance, a smaller market, lower margins, and is bought only by the customers the company cares about least. So every rational internal process shoots it down: listen to your best customers (they don't want it), rank projects by NPV (its numbers look ugly), protect your margin (this drags it down). Investing in the high-end upgrade for the mainstream is always "more worth it" than investing in the shabby low-end newcomer — and in the present moment, that judgment is correct every single time.

What's truly fatal is the crossing of two trajectories. Technology tends to improve faster than customer needs grow. The low-end disrupter at first can only serve the least demanding customers, but it climbs its own trajectory and sooner or later crosses the "good enough" line for mainstream customers — at which point its cheaper, simpler advantages all cash in, and the giant collapses at once. And what is the giant doing meanwhile? Happily fleeing upmarket, because the high end has fatter margins — fleeing until it has handed over the entire low and middle market. One climbs up, one flees up, the two lines cross in the mainstream, and the outcome is sealed. From disk drives to steel mini-mills to digital cameras, the same script replays. Christensen's remedy is therefore paradoxical: to survive, you must disrupt yourself with an independent small organization before others disrupt you — its own cost structure, its own customers, its own P&L — because a disruptive business can never survive under the parent's "correct" financial standards.

Key Quote
"The very decision-making and resource-allocation processes that are key to the success of established companies are the very processes that reject disruptive technologies."
— The Innovator's Dilemma, Introduction
Limits

Jill Lepore's famous 2014 critique in The New Yorker noted the theory leans heavily on cherry-picked retrospective cases — many "successful disruptions" are reverse-engineered, while failed would-be disrupters are ignored. It has strong explanatory power but weak predictive power: you can't reliably tell in advance which low-end product will actually succeed. Christensen himself later narrowed the boundaries of "disruption."

The Lean Startup
Eric Ries · 2011
Crown Business · ~336 pages
A startup isn't executing a plan — it's running experiments under extreme uncertainty, where the unit of progress is not features or revenue but what you learned.
What Makes This Book Distinctive

Ries's real insight isn't the everyone-says-it slogan "iterate fast." It's an unsettling diagnosis: in a startup, the vast majority of what looks like "progress" is an illusion. You built the features on plan, hit the milestones, even gathered some users — but if all of it rests on an unvalidated core assumption ("people will want this"), then you have merely, efficiently and on schedule, built something nobody wants. The traditional definition of productivity fails completely here: the question is not "did we build it" but "should we have built it, and on what basis are we so sure."

So he redefines progress as validated learning — the only progress that counts is using real data to prove your judgment about "do users actually want this" has moved closer to the truth. The MVP (minimum viable product) is widely misread because of this: it is not "a crude little product," it is the cheapest possible experimental apparatus for testing your riskiest assumption. A landing page, a fake "it works" video, a service that secretly fakes automation by hand — anything that forces a real user reaction is worth more than half a year of head-down development.

And Ries's most distinctive contribution is to confront a question others dodge: before you have any revenue, how do you know whether you're making progress? His answer is to distinguish "vanity metrics" from "actionable metrics" — total signups and cumulative downloads are sugar coating, curves that only go up, comforting but meaningless; what you should watch is cohort retention and the conversion funnel, which coldly tell you whether, after a new version, the behavior of new users actually got better. Without this "innovation accounting," so-called iteration is just self-deception: every loop must end with an honest verdict — pivot or persevere — and dodging that question makes even the fastest iteration spin in place.

Key Quote
"Validated learning is the process of demonstrating empirically that a team has discovered valuable truths about a startup's present and future business prospects."
— The Lean Startup, Chapter 3
Limits

The methodology easily ossifies into a new dogma — "test everything" barely applies to long-cycle, capital-heavy R&D fields (aerospace, semiconductors, biopharma), where forcing it dissolves real technical ambition. The MVP mindset has also led some teams to settle for "barely working" and call roughness a virtue.

Zero to One
Notes on Startups, or How to Build the Future · Peter Thiel & Blake Masters · 2014
Crown Business · ~210 pages
Everyone praises competition; Thiel says competition is exactly the destroyer of value — the real winner is the one who escapes it and builds a monopoly.
What Makes This Book Distinctive

Economics textbooks treat "perfect competition" as the ideal. Thiel's whole book is built on a flat reversal of this: under perfect competition, profit is zero. All players are ground down by price wars to bare survival, with no slack to invest in the long term. So any company that truly creates value and is worth founding is, in essence, pursuing some form of monopoly — through proprietary technology, network effects, economies of scale, or brand, becoming irreplaceable in its own lane. This is not a moral judgment but a structural fact: money and the room to innovate exist only outside of competition.

From this comes a sharp observation: monopolists and competitors both lie, but in opposite directions. A company stuck in a bloodbath frantically insists it is "unique" (defining its market narrowly, pretending to be the only one) to mask the truth that there's no profit; a real monopolist does the reverse, frantically downplaying its monopoly (defining the market broadly, stressing "we're only a few percent of it") to dodge regulators and envy. See through this camouflage and you can decode the rhetoric of a great many startup stories.

And Thiel's most distinctive — and most offensive — cut is aimed at the incrementalism the first three books share. He distinguishes "definite optimism" from "indefinite optimism": the former is holding a concrete, grand picture of the future in your mind and then building straight toward it; the latter is believing the future will be better without knowing what it is, so you dare only to keep options open and tweak in small steps. In his eyes lean iteration is exactly the latter — it can polish an existing thing to a local optimum but can never find the higher mountain, because a great company carries from day one a definite plan that others don't yet agree with. His signature interview question — "What important truth do very few people agree with you on?" — exists precisely to filter for people who dare hold a definite dissent: because everything that goes from zero to one begins with a secret almost nobody believed at the time.

Key Quote
"What important truth do very few people agree with you on?"
— Zero to One, Preface (the question that organizes the whole book)
Limits

Intensely autobiographical — the theory rests almost entirely on PayPal, Palantir, and a handful of Founders Fund bets, a tiny sample with serious survivorship bias. The case for monopoly slides easily into impatience with antitrust and thin attention to social externalities. The prose carries a strong ideology, and many of its conclusions readers will not share.

Questions to Ask Yourself

  1. For what you're building, are the "early customers" buying it and the "mainstream customers" you want to win the two different kinds of people Moore describes? Where exactly is the chasm between you and the latter stuck — product completeness, peer references, or something else?
  2. In your industry, what unglamorous low-end product is nibbling at the incumbent giant? Christensen's point isn't "there's a rival" but "the giant's rational processes make it unable to fight back" — does that hold in your company?
  3. Your most recent "milestone hit": did it validate "we can build it" or "users really want it"? Are you betting the same amount on those two?
  4. The core metric you watch now — is it a Ries vanity metric (only goes up, feels good) or an actionable one (tells you whether the new version actually got better)?
  5. Use Thiel's prompt: what important truth do you believe that your peers and investors almost universally disagree with you on? If you can't answer, does that mean you're building 1→n or 0→1?
