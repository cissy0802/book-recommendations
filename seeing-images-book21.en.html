Seeing & Images

We assume seeing is neutral. In fact, every act of looking has been trained, shaped, and designed. These four books each pry open one stage of "seeing."

2026 · Book Recommendations · No. 21

Introduction

Looking seems like the most natural thing in the world. Each of these four books seizes one overlooked mechanism within it: Berger shows that seeing is never neutral—it is arranged in advance by our knowledge, interests, and ideology; Sontag dissects the act of the camera itself—taking a photograph is a form of appropriation and aggression; Tufte gives an engineering standard for "how to display data without lying"; and Gombrich reduces the whole of art history to generations of people solving the inherited problem of how to see and how to depict. By the end, you will distrust, a little more usefully, what your eyes report.

The Four Books in Detail

Ways of Seeing
Ways of Seeing · John Berger · 1972
Penguin Books (based on the 1972 BBC television series) · ~166 pp
Before you can name what is in front of you, you have already seen it—and how you see it was arranged in advance by your situation and the beliefs instilled in you.
Core Insight

Berger opens with a line that overturns common sense: seeing comes before words, yet seeing is never neutral reception. What we see is never "the thing itself" but the relation between things and ourselves—your knowledge, beliefs, class, and era have filtered it before you are aware. "Objective seeing" is an illusion.

He uses oil painting to prove it. What recurs in the European oil tradition is not beauty but possession: landscapes are real estate, still lifes are wealth, portraits are status. Oil paint excels at the surface texture of objects—silk, gold, fur—because it is essentially in the service of displaying ownership. The viewer is assumed to be the proprietor.

Sharpest is his reading of the female nude. Berger distinguishes being naked (being oneself) from the nude (an object to be looked at). The person who is absent yet decides everything in a traditional nude is the male spectator outside the frame—the woman's pose and gaze are arranged to please him. Hence the famous formulation: men act and women appear. From childhood a woman learns to be at once the surveyed and the one surveying herself being surveyed.

The book's final blow lands on publicity (advertising). Berger calls it the contemporary heir to oil painting's logic, but with the tense reversed: oil painting displays what you already own; publicity sells what you are about to own. Its core operation is not to make you desire the product but to manufacture envy—to make you dissatisfied with your present self and promise that, once you buy, you will become the person others envy. "The state of being envied is what constitutes glamour." That mechanism lives, unchanged, in every feed today.

Key Quotes
"Seeing comes before words. The child looks and recognizes before it can speak."

"We never look at just one thing; we are always looking at the relation between things and ourselves."
— Ways of Seeing, Chapter 1
"Men act and women appear. Men look at women. Women watch themselves being looked at."

Limitations

A slim 1972 book—incisive but extremely compact; many claims are asserted rather than argued, more manifesto than systematic aesthetics. Strongly shaped by a Marxist frame, it reduces nearly all images to class and ownership, treating the religious, formal, and purely aesthetic dimensions thinly.

For BigCat

Berger's "publicity manufactures envy" is the best entry point for teaching a school-age child media literacy. Your child is soaked daily in images from short-video apps, and almost all of them run Berger's program: make you dissatisfied, then promise "buy it / play it and you'll become the envied one." One concrete thing to try this week: sit with your child and dissect three ads or product-pushing clips she has scrolled past, asking only two questions of each—"What does it want you to feel you're missing right now?" and "Who does it promise you'll become if you buy it?" The aim isn't prohibition; it's installing Berger's "seeing through" into her eyes early.

On Photography
On Photography · Susan Sontag · 1977
Farrar, Straus and Giroux · ~207 pp (six essays)
The camera looks like mere recording; in fact every press of the shutter is an act of possession, an intervention—and the more images there are, the duller our capacity to feel.
Core Insight

Sontag redefines an act considered innocent—taking a photograph—as a relation of power. "To collect photographs is to collect the world." Photographing is not passive reflection but appropriation of its subject: turning the living and flowing into a fragment one can own, collect, and archive. The camera gives the illusion that the world is "within one's grasp."

From this comes her merciless line: there is an aggression implicit in every use of the camera. To photograph a person is to objectify them in a way they can never see of themselves; to aim a camera at something is to intervene—gentle on the surface, yet a kind of "soft murder," fitting an anxious age desperate to grab hold of something. The tourist raising a camera often does so to relieve the unease of "not knowing how to face what's in front of me": photograph it, and one need not really be present.

Deeper still is the inversion of image and experience. We increasingly confirm the reality of an experience through its photograph—if it wasn't shot, it's as if it didn't happen; travel slowly becomes "collecting evidence for the album." Reworking Mallarmé, she pins down her generation's condition: once everything existed to end in a book; today everything exists to end in a photograph.

Most prescient is her account of the ethical fallout of image glut. Sontag feared that as suffering, violence, and catastrophe are endlessly shot and circulated, the first sight may move us but the thousandth merely numbs. Images meant to rouse conscience, in excess, corrode feeling—a prediction in 1977, a daily reality in today's feed. What she demands is an ethics of seeing: clarity about what we look at and why.

Key Quotes
"To collect photographs is to collect the world."
— On Photography, opening of "In Plato's Cave"
"To photograph is to appropriate the thing photographed."
— On Photography, "In Plato's Cave"
"There is an aggression implicit in every use of the camera."
— On Photography, "In Plato's Cave"
Limitations

Written in the film-and-magazine era, it argues about a world of scarce, developed photographs; smartphones—everyone shooting and posting instantly—require recalibrating many of its pessimistic verdicts. The aphoristic essay form is dazzling but light on systematic argument, and is often criticized for treating photography wholesale as predation, with too little on its cognitive and emancipatory potential.

For BigCat

Sontag's "today everything exists to end in a photograph" cuts deepest for a mother used to documenting her child's growth. We capture her every moment, yet—busy framing, composing, posting—we are often not truly present; the camera becomes a shield against directly facing the moment. One reverse experiment this week: pick a full outing with your child (an hour or two) and take not a single photo, swapping the urge to "leave evidence" for "being fully here." Afterward, notice: is the memory without photographs vaguer—or actually clearer, and more truly yours? This is exactly what Sontag asks us to distinguish: the experience and its image were never the same thing.

The Visual Display of Quantitative Information
The Visual Display of Quantitative Information · Edward R. Tufte · 1983 (2nd ed. 2001)
Graphics Press · ~200 pp
From one set of data, one chart makes the truth obvious and another distorts it utterly—the difference is not taste but a measurable engineering standard.
Core Insight

Where the first two books dissect how images deceive, Tufte asks the reverse: what makes a chart honest and powerful? His answer is not a matter of taste but a near-engineering standard. First iron law: above all else, show the data. A graphic exists solely to let data speak; anything that grabs attention without carrying data is noise.

He coined two widely cited concepts. One is the data-ink ratio: as much of a chart's ink as possible should depict the data itself, not borders, shading, 3-D shadows, or decorative gridlines. The other is chartjunk: visual noise piled on for "looks" that actually lowers information. Tufte's stance is nearly austere—erase until you cannot erase further; every drop of ink must answer for understanding.

Data-Ink Ratio · Same Data, Two Renderings

But Tufte is no advocate of the threadbare. His benchmark is the 19th-century French engineer Minard's map of Napoleon's Russian campaign—a single graphic showing the army's route, direction, dwindling numbers as it advances, geographic coordinates, and temperature: six variables, an entire catastrophe compressed into one still image. Tufte calls it what "may well be the best statistical graphic ever drawn." Excellence is not bareness but conveying the greatest number of ideas with the least ink.

He also supplies a tool to quantify lying: the Lie Factor = size of effect shown in the graphic ÷ size of effect in the data. The ideal is 1. When a chart uses area or volume to represent a one-dimensional number (doubling a value but drawing an icon whose side doubles and area quadruples), the Lie Factor balloons—the chart exaggerates on your behalf. It gives us a ruler to measure how much any chart magnifies or shrinks the truth.

Key Quotes
"Graphical excellence is that which gives to the viewer the greatest number of ideas in the shortest time with the least ink in the smallest space."
— The Visual Display of Quantitative Information, "Graphical Excellence"
"Above all else show the data."
— The Visual Display of Quantitative Information, "Theory of Data Graphics"
Limitations

Written before interactive visualization, it treats almost exclusively static print graphics and has nothing to say about today's dynamic, zoomable, clickable data interfaces. Tufte's minimalism can verge on dogma—chasing the highest data-ink ratio may strip away the guidance and context ordinary readers need; studies also suggest that modest "decoration" can improve recall and comprehension for non-expert readers.

For BigCat

Tufte lands squarely on the dashboards and presentation charts a technologist handles daily. This week: take one key-metric or model-evaluation chart your team looks at often, and audit it with Tufte's two rulers—(1) data-ink ratio: after deleting the 3-D effects, gradient fills, surplus gridlines, and legend boxes, is any information lost? If not, they were chartjunk all along. (2) Lie Factor: does the y-axis start at 0? Has a truncated axis turned a 2% wobble into a "cliff"? Then, against Minard's standard, ask: could this chart, like that one, honestly pack in one more useful dimension on a single screen (e.g., overlaying last year's same period, or a confidence band) instead of piling on decoration?

The Story of Art
The Story of Art · E. H. Gombrich · 1950 (16th ed. 1995)
Phaidon Press · ~688 pp
Art history is not a temple enshrining geniuses but a relay—each generation of artists working on the "how to see, how to depict" problems the last one left unsolved.
Core Insight

This globally bestselling introduction—over eight million copies—opens by dismantling the reader's awe: there really is no such thing as Art, there are only artists. Gombrich refuses to enshrine capital-A "Art" as an idol and intimidating mystique; he sends us back to specific people, specific situations, and the specific problems they were trying to solve.

What he offers is not a checklist of movement-names but a continuous story line: each generation works atop the achievements and unsolved problems the last one left. The Egyptians "drew what they knew" (figures rendered most recognizably—a frontal eye on a profile face); the Greeks began to "draw what they saw," cracking perspective and foreshortening; the Renaissance solved spatial depth and anatomy; the Impressionists overturned "painting by knowledge" and returned to honestly painting the light and color the eye receives in this instant. Each breakthrough answers the problem the previous generation left, not a genius striking from nowhere.

With this Gombrich dissolves the two commonest obstacles a layperson faces. One is "I don't understand it, so I daren't have an opinion"—he says there is no wrong reason for liking a picture; what to watch for is the wrong dislike: rejecting a work because its subject makes you uncomfortable, or because it doesn't match your preconception of "what a picture should look like." The other is mistaking the unfamiliar for the inferior—he explains tribal masks, medieval icons, and Renaissance masterpieces with equal patience, showing that every work should be measured against the problem it set out to solve, not held to a single "does it look like a photograph?" ruler.

His deeper contribution: he turns "reading a picture" into a learnable skill rather than a gift. When you look at a painting and ask "what effect was this painter after, which problem did he solve that his predecessors hadn't," you upgrade from passive "nice / not nice" to an active mode of inquiry—dovetailing with the issue's first three books: seeing can be trained to be more clear-eyed.

Key Quotes
"There really is no such thing as Art. There are only artists."
— The Story of Art, opening of the Introduction
"There is no wrong reason for liking a statue or a picture."
— The Story of Art, Introduction
Limitations

First published in 1950, its narrative weight sits almost entirely on the West (especially Europe), treating Chinese, Indian, Islamic, and African art thinly—hence the charge of being "Western-centric." Its story frame, threaded by "progress in representation," loses explanatory power for late-20th-century conceptual, installation, and performance art—once art no longer chases "looking more lifelike," the progress narrative struggles to absorb it.

For BigCat

What truly transfers from Gombrich is his method of reading history, not the art knowledge itself—and that is gold for "AI super-individual"-style cross-disciplinary learning. Most people learn a new field (neuroscience, complexity science, quantum mechanics) by memorizing a string of masters and terms, ending up with a static temple. Gombrich's method inverts this: read any field's history as a relay of problem-solving—which specific problem left by predecessors did each key figure solve? This week: pick a field you're working through, draw a timeline, and at each node write only one sentence—"the unsolved problem he inherited was X, his breakthrough was Y, and he left Z for those after him." When the names become a causal chain, what you retain is not isolated conclusions but the field's actual mode of motion—and you'll more readily spot where the next unsolved "Z" lies, and whether it's exactly where you could enter with AI.

Questions to Ask Yourself

  1. An image you saw in your feed today—was it letting you "see the world," or, in Berger's sense, manufacturing dissatisfaction with yourself, making you envious? What did it promise you'd "become once you own it"?
    A frame to judge by

    Use Berger's two questions as probes: (1) What does this image want me to feel I'm missing right now? (2) Is the "post-purchase me" it promises a figure others would envy? If you can answer both, you weren't seeing information but publicity—glamour is "the state of being envied" turned into a commodity. Asking these two lines turns you from the image's object back into its examiner.

  2. The last time you raised your phone to shoot—was it to truly remember the moment, or, as Sontag says, to "avoid directly facing it," shooting to collect evidence? Does an experience you didn't photograph feel, to you, as if it didn't happen?
    A frame to judge by

    Run a discrimination test: recall your last trip or important occasion—are there more details you "remember in your body," or more you "only remember through photos"? If almost all memory needs the album to be retrieved, the camera has been "experiencing" that time for you. Sontag isn't against photographing; she wants clarity: shooting and being present are two different ways of living—don't let the former quietly eat the latter.

  3. The last chart you made (a report, dashboard, slide)—how high is its data-ink ratio? If you deleted every element not carrying data, would it get clearer or weaker? Is its Lie Factor 1?
    A frame to judge by

    Run Tufte's checklist item by item: 3-D effects, gradients, shadows, surplus gridlines, decorative icons—delete each and ask "did information shrink?" If not, it's chartjunk. Then check whether the y-axis starts at 0, whether a truncated axis magnifies a wobble, whether area/volume is used to depict a one-dimensional value (the Lie Factor's danger zone). An honest, good chart should be like Minard's: ink minimal, dimensions many, and the size of the effect faithful to the size of the data.
