The Wellsprings of the Chinese Classics

How to be a person, how to win inner freedom, how to read history, how to voice emotion — these "default settings" still run quietly in the background today. Which source-texts first wrote them down?

2026 · Book Recommendations · No. 54

Introduction

You can skip "the classics" and still never escape these four books — they long ago sank into the bedrock of the Chinese language. The Analects writes in the idea that a person becomes a person only within relationships, grounding ethics in concrete acts you can practice daily. Zhuangzi dismantles the rulers of big-and-small, useful-and-useless, right-and-wrong, loosening the mind from those scales first. The Records of the Grand Historian builds its history out of individual biographies, showing that to read history is to read how people choose. The Book of Songs supplies the original grammar of Chinese emotion — not abstract cries, but feeling evoked through a bird, a leaf, a fall of rain. Each lays down one channel; this issue traces the mechanism at each source.

The Four Books in Detail

The Analects
Lúnyǔ · Confucius & his disciples (compiled by later followers) · late Spring & Autumn – early Warring States
20 chapters · c. 16,000 characters · Yang Bojun's annotated edition is the standard modern text
It offers no system, only a scatter of remarks made to fit the moment — because "how to be a person" can never be settled once and for all, only practiced again and again in the particular case.
The Core Insight

The Analects is easily misread as a collection of moral maxims. Its true form is the recorded conversation: to the same word ren, Confucius gives completely different answers to different students. This is not confusion but the point itself — being a person is not reciting one universal definition, but getting the measure right within a concrete relationship and situation. Who is asking, and what their weakness is, changes the answer — this is teaching adapted to the person.

The hinge of the whole book is ren (humaneness), and its workable entry point is shu (reciprocity): "Do not impose on others what you yourself do not desire." What is remarkable is that the rule is stated negatively — it does not tell you to define the good for others, only to refrain from imposing on them what you would not bear yourself. It presupposes no lofty "ought," but sets the reference point back inside your own felt experience, which is why it holds across any age and any relationship. This "way of shu" is widely recognized as one of humanity's earliest formulations of the Golden Rule.

A second, underrated mechanism is ritual (li). Today the word suggests empty formality, but for Confucius, ritual is the channel that stably externalizes inner feeling — without form, goodwill scatters and distorts. Mourning rites give grief a place to rest; the measured gestures of greeting make respect perceptible. Hence his line, "If a person is not humane, what has he to do with ritual?" — mere form without true feeling is a husk, but conversely, feeling without form cannot land or travel between people. Ritual and ren are two sides of one thing.

Further still is the rectification of names — "let the ruler be a ruler, the minister a minister, the father a father, the son a son." This is no conservative sermon on staying in one's place, but a sharp diagnosis: when the responsibilities owed under a role are hollowed out and only the title and its power remain, the relationship begins to rot. A father unlike a father, a superior who won't carry a superior's duty — this is where order starts to collapse. Confucius nails ethics firmly to relationship: a person is never first an isolated self who then relates to others; a person grows, bit by bit, into a person only within the relations of father and son, friend and friend, ruler and subject.

Key Lines
"Do not impose on others what you yourself do not desire."

"When you know a thing, to hold that you know it; and when you do not know a thing, to allow that you do not — this is knowledge."

"The noble person seeks harmony, not sameness; the small person seeks sameness, not harmony."

Limitations

The recorded-saying form lacks systematic argument; the same claim is scattered across chapters, needing the reader to piece it together and easily quoted out of context. Its ethics assume a framework of hierarchy and kinship (e.g., filial duty above individual choice) that sits in tension with modern values of equality and autonomy — one must tell the transferable core from the period-bound shell.

For BigCat

"Rectifying names" cuts sharpest for leading a team. When a team turns chaotic, it is usually not a competence problem but that some role's title still stands while the duty behind it has been quietly emptied out — a "lead" who makes no decisions, a "reviewer" who won't own a veto. One thing to try this week: take the team's role list and ask of each seat a single question — "What is the duty owed under this name that, right now, no one is carrying?" Restoring it works faster than reorganizing the chart. And shu is the most economical rule of collaboration: before pushing any process, demand, or overtime onto a colleague, run it through "what I would not want myself" — do not assume others should bear what you would refuse. That one negative check beats a whole seminar on values.

Zhuangzi
Zhuāngzǐ · Zhuang Zhou & his school · Warring States
Inner Chapters (7) / Outer / Miscellaneous · c. 65,000 characters · Guo Qingfan's collated edition is standard
It does not urge you to fight for a better position; it makes you doubt "position" itself — every scale of big and small, noble and base, useful and useless: cut by whom, and from what standpoint?
The Core Insight

If the Analects teaches you to weave yourself into relations, Zhuangzi asks the reverse: the marks on that net — by what right? It opens not with argument but with an image: the Peng, a fish thousands of miles long that becomes a bird and flies ninety thousand miles up. The little cicada and dove laugh — why fly so high? Through this picture Zhuangzi makes his point: every being measures others by its own scale, and takes that scale for the order of nature itself.

This leads to the central chapter, "On the Equality of Things." "Equalizing things" does not claim that all things are truly the same, but that judgments of right and wrong, beautiful and ugly, big and small all depend on an unspoken standpoint — change the standpoint and the verdict flips. A person sleeping in the damp gets a sore back; a loach does not. Everyone calls the legendary beauties lovely, yet fish dive deep and birds fly high at the sight of them. So "who is truly beautiful?" has no answer from any standpoint outside a standpoint. Zhuangzi is not pushing you toward "anything goes" nihilism, but dismantling the dogma that "only my ruler is the correct one" — once you see the marks were marked on, the mind slips its bonds.

From here he inverts the word everyone chases: usefulness. People strive to be "useful"; Zhuangzi keeps praising "the use of the useless" — the gnarled, twisted tree the carpenter scorns is, precisely because useless, never felled, and grows into a giant shading a whole area. This is no call to laziness, but a reminder: "useful" is always relative to some external purpose, and when you stake your entire worth on being "useful to others," you hand over the very power to define yourself.

The deepest layer is transformation and death. Zhuang Zhou dreams he is a butterfly, and on waking cannot tell whether Zhou dreamed the butterfly or the butterfly dreams Zhou — not a word-game, but a loosening of "I," that seemingly firmest of boundaries. So when his wife dies he drums on a basin and sings: life and death are but the gathering and scattering of qi, one great transformation, like the turning of the seasons. This equanimity is not coldness but the freeing of the individual from the fixation of "I must forever be this I, must clutch and never let go." What Zhuangzi offers is the ability, under any discipline or any single system of appraisal, to keep for yourself a private plot of spirit.

Key Lines
"My life has a limit, but knowledge has none. To pursue the limitless with the limited is perilous."
— Zhuangzi, "Nourishing the Lord of Life"
"You are not a fish — how do you know what fish enjoy?"
— Zhuangzi, "Autumn Floods" (the debate on the bridge over the Hao)
"When the springs dry up and the fish are stranded together on land, moistening each other with breath and spittle — better they had forgotten each other in the rivers and lakes."
— Zhuangzi, "The Great and Venerable Teacher"
Limitations

The style is vast and unbridled, working through parable rather than argument, and lends itself to flatly opposite readings — taken both as world-fleeing quietism and as active worldly wisdom. Grasp only its "seeing through the scales" carelessly and you slide toward cynicism or evasion of real duty; it gives the capacity for inner freedom, not the answer to how to land it within relationship and responsibility — for that half, you return to the Analects.

For BigCat

Zhuangzi's "equalizing things" is a remedy for identity anxiety in the AI age. Tech people are often held hostage by a single scale — raise size, title level, who adopted the new model faster — and the deeper that ruler cuts, the more anxious one grows. One exercise to try this week: write down the thing you are anxious about ("I use AI less fluently than my colleague," "I've fallen behind this cycle"), then ask — "Whose ruler is this? Does it still hold under a different one?" Measure the same thing by "will it matter in three years?" or "does it matter for the deep fields I truly care about?" and it usually loosens. And "the use of the useless" applies directly to a child: don't pack every stretch of her time with "useful," credential-bearing lessons — deliberately leave a block of purposeless blank: daydreaming, aimless play, watching ants. That is exactly the "uselessness" Zhuangzi says a great tree needs in order to grow.

Records of the Grand Historian
Shǐjì · Sima Qian · Han · completed c. 91 BCE
130 chapters (Annals / Tables / Treatises / Hereditary Houses / Biographies) · c. 526,000 characters · Zhonghua Book Company collated edition
It invented a way of seeing history still in use today: not bookkeeping by year, but a biography for one person after another — because history's true protagonist is how a person chooses within a situation.
The Core Insight

The greatest originality of the Records is its structure itself. Before it, histories were largely year-by-year ledgers (like the Spring and Autumn Annals). Sima Qian turned the frame from "time" to "person" — founding the biographical form: annals for emperors, hereditary houses for feudal lords, and biographies for figures of every kind. Behind this inversion is a view of history: history is not an abstract tide moving on its own, but individual people making choices within concrete situations, which converge into the tide. To understand an age, you must first understand the people inside it.

More striking is whom he chose to memorialize. Assassins, wandering knights, merchants, jesters, diviners — the marginal figures official history always disdained to record, Sima Qian gravely gave biographies. Yu Rang and Nie Zheng in the "Biographies of Assassins," Guo Jie among the wandering knights, are no emperors or generals. What he set out to write is the weight and dignity of people across every way of living, not only success and failure at the summit of power. This gaze that "keeps a name for the nameless" is why the Records still moves readers two thousand years on.

He also charged history with a scalding attitude. The "the Grand Historian remarks" that closes each chapter is Sima Qian stepping in directly to appraise — admiration, regret, and anger undisguised. Of Li Guang he sighs, "The peach and plum trees do not speak, yet a path forms beneath them," crying out for a great general who never won a fief; of Xiang Yu, a loser, he nonetheless writes an "annal," a form reserved for emperors, because in his eyes Xiang Yu truly ruled an era. He judges history's real weight, not the official verdict of victor and vanquished.

Beneath all this is the highest standard he set himself: "To examine the boundary between Heaven and man, to comprehend the changes of past and present, and to complete the words of a single school." Suffering the disgrace of castration, he endured to finish the book precisely because he judged there was something weightier than personal honor to leave behind. So his line, "A man has only one death — it may be weightier than Mount Tai or lighter than a goose feather," is no maxim but a pledge he redeemed with the whole second half of his life.

Key Lines
"To examine the boundary between Heaven and man, to comprehend the changes of past and present, and to complete the words of a single school."
— Sima Qian, "Letter in Reply to Ren An" (his stated aim in writing history)
"A man has only one death. That death may be weightier than Mount Tai, or lighter than a goose feather."
— Sima Qian, "Letter in Reply to Ren An"
"The peach and plum trees do not speak, yet a path forms beneath them."
— Records of the Grand Historian, "Biography of General Li" (the Grand Historian's remark)
Limitations

Sima Qian's strong personal praise and blame are both his charm and a risk — his loves and hatreds shape what he selects and how he trims, so the reader must separate fact from the historian's verdict. The high-antiquity portions (e.g., the "Annals of the Five Emperors") blend in legend; their dates and sources are much revised by modern scholarship. The sheer bulk and difficult classical prose make it nearly unreadable without a good annotated edition.

For BigCat

Sima Qian's "people as the axis" gives investing and reading character a hard method. Financials, sectors, grand narratives are the "year-ledger"; what usually decides outcomes is a key figure's choice at a key moment. To try this week: for the founder/CEO of a company you hold heavily, write a two-hundred-word "biography" by hand — which choices did they make in past adversity, and what character did those reveal? This predicts their next move better than another ten research reports. And "the Grand Historian remarks" is a superb review template: after each important decision (an investment, a hire, a tech choice), write, as Sima Qian did, a closing "so-and-so remarks" — not a running log, but your true judgment of and feeling about the thing. Over a year, this private, opinionated set of biographies will show you your own decision patterns better than any KPI report.

The Book of Songs
Shījīng · Anonymous (gathered in the Zhou, transmitted via Confucius) · c. 11th – 6th century BCE
305 poems (Airs / Odes / Hymns) · c. 39,000 characters · Cheng Junying's annotated edition is the standard modern text
China's earliest poetry begins not with "I am so sad" but with "that water-bird is calling," "those willow leaves drift" — the original grammar of feeling is to entrust the heart to a concrete thing before your eyes.
The Core Insight

The Book of Songs is the headwater of all Chinese literature — 305 poems in three classes: Airs (regional folk songs), Odes (works of the officer class), and Hymns (temple sacrificial songs). What it truly established is a method of voicing emotion that has lasted three thousand years, later summed up as "narrate, compare, stir." To narrate is to state directly; to compare is to liken; the most distinctive — and most "Chinese" — is xing, to stir.

Xing means naming first a seemingly unrelated object before you, then letting the feeling rise out of it. "Guan-guan cry the ospreys, on the islet in the river" — first a pair of birds calling on a river islet, and only then "the gentle, graceful lady is a fine match for the gentleman." Between the birds and the courtship there is no logical proof, yet a natural emotional linkage. This is the original grammar of Chinese lyric: not shouting the inner state outright, but entrusting feeling to an outer thing and letting the thing speak. Every later tradition of "writing feeling through scene," of "borrowing an object to voice the heart," has its source here.

Its second power is concreteness. The Book of Songs almost never uses abstract grand words; it is all tangible, sensible detail. To write the pain of parting it does not say "I am so miserable," but — "When I set out long ago, the willows softly swayed; now as I return, the snow falls thick": leaving in spring with tender willow branches, coming back amid drifting snow. The cruelty of time, the vicissitudes of human affairs, are pressed entirely into the contrast of two sets of scenery, without one abstract word, yet weightier than any outcry. This is the Book of Songs' most precious bequest to Chinese: hold the deepest feeling in the most concrete thing.

Worth noting too is its sound. The Songs are mostly four-syllable lines, dense with reduplicated words and repeated stanzas — "guan-guan," "swaying," "thick-falling" — a single sigh drawn out over three refrains, the same passage sung again with a few words changed. They were songs set to music and sung, not texts read silently at a desk. This reminds us that poetry's source is bodily, oral, made to be sung and chanted: feeling enters the heart first through the rhythm of sound, and only then through meaning.

Key Lines
"When I set out long ago, the willows were softly swaying; now as I come back, the snow falls thick upon the rain."
— The Book of Songs, "Lesser Odes," Cai Wei
"Guan-guan cry the ospreys, on the islet in the river. The gentle, graceful lady is a fine match for the gentleman."
— The Book of Songs, "Airs of Zhounan," Guan Ju (the opening poem; the model of xing)
"Stones from another hill may serve to polish this one's jade."
— The Book of Songs, "Lesser Odes," He Ming
Limitations

Remote in time and archaic in diction, it is hard to read without annotation; and generations of classical scholarship (above all the Han "Mao Preface") forced what were songs of love and labor into political-moral allegory — the reader must peel off this later coat of morality to touch the poems' living freshness. It gives the grammar of lyric feeling, not a model for narrative or reasoning — for those, look elsewhere.

For BigCat

"Stirring" and "concreteness" are the best tools for a child's emotional education. Children (and adults) often cannot articulate feeling and only cry "I'm bored," "this is pointless" — because naming emotion directly is hard. Borrow the Book of Songs' path: rather than pressing her to say "what do you feel," start from a concrete thing before her — "the clouds moved so fast today, like they were chasing something?" Stir from the object, and the feeling flows out. A small game to try this week: at bedtime, each of you says one line — "what weather / what color was today like" — using a concrete image in place of the abstract "happy/sad." This is both the Book of Songs' "xing" and a quiet lesson in something useful for life — entrusting the heart you cannot speak to a concrete thing that can be seen. The same craft serves your own writing and speech: fewer adjectives, one more image.

A Few Questions to Ask Yourself

  1. Hold your team or family up to the Analects' "rectification of names": is there a role whose title and power both remain, but whose real duty is, in fact, no longer being carried by anyone?
    A frame for judging

    Ask each role two things: what is this seat "nominally" responsible for? And "in reality," on whom does that duty now fall — or is it hanging in the air? Once the two stay misaligned for long, order starts to rot right there — and the fix often needs no reorganization, only nailing the emptied duty back onto its name. If you can point to "which name, missing which duty," you're already ahead of most reviews.

  2. Your most recent bout of anxiety — by whose ruler was it measured? Swap in a Zhuangzi ruler (will it matter in three years? does it matter for the fields I truly care about?) — does it still hold?
    A frame for judging

    Zhuangzi doesn't ask you to deny all standards, but to see clearly: every judgment of "I'm not good enough" presupposes a ruler. First name that ruler — is it salary, title, "faster with AI than a colleague"? Then ask whether it is the one correct measure, and whether you genuinely endorse it. Most anxiety comes not from your really being worse, but from defaulting to a ruler someone handed you that you don't actually accept.

  3. Try to voice your present mood the Book of Songs' way: no abstract emotion words allowed (happy, sad, anxious) — only a single concrete object before your eyes. Can you do it?
    A frame for judging

    This is not a word-game but a check-up on your capacity to express. If, without labels like "I'm so tired / so annoyed," you can't state a feeling, it means you're used to tagging emotion rather than truly regarding it. And the path of xing — starting from a cloud, a fall of rain, a cup of tea gone cold — often lands closer to the real inner state than any label. The more precise the concrete thing you write, the nearer you are to your own emotion.
