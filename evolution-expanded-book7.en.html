Issue 7 · Themed Reading

The Expanding Universe of Evolution

After Darwin, the live argument was never "does natural selection exist," but "what is the unit being selected" — genes, behaviors, symbiotic mergers, or whole lineages constrained by historical contingency? Four books spanning 1976 to 1998 record the sharpest debate before the field moved toward the Extended Synthesis.

2026 · Bidaily Books · Issue 7

Why these four together

This issue is not "reading evolution." It is reading the dispute over the unit of selection. In 1976 Dawkins pushed the unit to the smallest scale: genes are the true replicators; organisms are their "survival machines." Wilson extended the gene-centric story from insect societies to human behavior and culture, founding the field of sociobiology that the left would attack for decades. Margulis opened the question from the opposite end: eukaryotic cells are themselves mergers of multiple prokaryotic ancestors — the pivotal events in evolution were not competition but fusion. Gould, standing on the side of paleontology, threw cold water: selection is half the story; the other half is historical contingency and structural constraint. Not every trait is "optimized."

All four accept that natural selection is real. But their answers to "which level is being selected" are almost mutually incompatible. That incompatibility is the signature of a living scientific field, not a defect. After this set you will know what actual argument lies behind the single word "evolution."

The four books in detail

The Selfish Gene
Richard Dawkins · 1976
Oxford University Press · about 360 pages (30th-anniversary edition adds two prefaces and extended endnotes)
Pushes the unit of natural selection from the organism down to the gene. A book written for non-specialists that simultaneously reshaped specialist debate.
Core claims
  • The gene (replicator) is the real unit of selection. A peacock's tail exists not because it benefits the peacock but because ancestors carrying "grow-a-big-tail" genes left, on average, more copies of those genes in the next generation. The organism is a "vehicle" for genes; if it dies, that's fine — the genes carry on inside other vehicles.
  • The evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS). Game theory enters evolutionary biology in a serious way. Once a strategy reaches certain frequencies in a population, no alternative can invade. The "hawk vs. dove" distribution in animal behavior is a mathematical equilibrium, not a moral choice.
  • "Selfish" is a mathematical description, not a moral judgment. Dawkins clarifies this repeatedly in later prefaces, but the misreading endures. It refers to the cold process of gene-frequency maximization, not the psychology of any organism.
  • The meme concept is born in the closing chapter. Culture has replicators too — ideas, religions, catchphrases spread by similar evolutionary logic. This single chapter spawned later work on cultural evolution and the term "internet meme."
Notable quotes
"We are survival machines—robot vehicles blindly programmed to preserve the selfish molecules known as genes."
— The Selfish Gene, 1976 Preface
"Be warned that if you wish, as I do, to build a society in which individuals cooperate generously and unselfishly towards a common good, you can expect little help from biological nature."
— The Selfish Gene, Chapter 1 "Why are people?"
Conversation with the other three

This book is the main target for both Margulis and Gould in this issue. Margulis would say: "Your gene-centric view cannot explain the origin of the eukaryotic cell — that was not competition between genes, it was a merger between cells." Gould would say: "You treat every trait as an optimized adaptation and ignore developmental constraint and historical path-dependence." Wilson is an ally and a divergent partner: both stand for gene-centrism, but Wilson pushed it directly into human social behavior — a step from which Dawkins himself later kept some distance.

Limits and controversy

The word "selfish" produced widespread misreading. Dawkins himself admits in the 30th-anniversary preface that the title would have been more accurate as The Immortal Gene. The total rejection of group selection may be too strong — later Wilson, David Sloan Wilson, and others rehabilitated multilevel selection. The book is reductionist in temperament: explaining evolution as gene mathematics, it pays little attention to development, epigenetics, and ecological networks — the "middle layers." Those gaps are exactly what the other three books in this issue try to fill.

On Human Nature
E. O. Wilson · 1978
Harvard University Press · about 280 pages · Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction, 1979
Applies sociobiology to human beings. Wilson's 1975 Sociobiology had pushed the framework from insects to humans in its final chapter and triggered enormous controversy; this book is the defense, extension, and systematization for general readers.
Core claims
  • Human emotions and moral tendencies are also shaped by natural selection. Aggression, altruism, sex roles, and religion all have evolutionary-genetic explanations — they are not pure cultural construction. This was the most provocative and most explanatory claim of 1978.
  • "Genes hold culture on a leash." Genes do not directly determine every behavior, but they shape dispositions — how language is acquired, what triggers fear, why incest taboos appear — and thus constrain the space of possible cultures. Culture can roam far, but the leash eventually pulls back.
  • Religion is an evolutionary product. Wilson treats religious belief as a byproduct of group-cohesion mechanisms, closely tied to cooperation and warfare. Writing this in 1978 took courage.
  • The temptation of determinism must be taken seriously. Wilson warns again and again that sociobiology is not racism and not "genes decide everything," but he also refuses to abandon the research framework because of those risks. This walk-and-disclaim posture is the most exhausting part of the book — and the most intellectually honest.
Notable quotes
"The genes hold culture on a leash. The leash is very long, but inevitably values will be constrained in accordance with their effects on the human gene pool."
— On Human Nature, Chapter 7 "Religion"
"Human emotional responses and the more general ethical practices based on them have been programmed to a substantial degree by natural selection over thousands of generations."
— On Human Nature, Chapter 1 "Dilemma"
Conversation with the other three

Aligned with Dawkins on gene-centrism, but extending in a different direction: Dawkins cares about the ontological identity of the replicator, while Wilson cares about how genes shape social behavior. Directly opposed by Gould — Gould and Lewontin's famous 1979 "Spandrels of San Marco" paper was written partly to reject sociobiology, and this issue's Gould volume is the popular-science continuation of that fight. Almost on a different plane from Margulis — Margulis cares about the merger-history of biological systems, while Wilson cares about the gene-explanation of individual behavior. They do not directly debate, but you will notice that Wilson's "genes → society" pipeline leaves no room at all for symbiotic evolution.

Limits and controversy

The "genes vs. culture" dichotomy looks too clean today; gene-culture coevolution is the more refined frame, which Wilson himself later absorbed. The evidence base for evolutionary explanations of human behavior in 1978 was much thinner than today, and many conclusions rest on a "if this were an adaptation it would make sense" logic — exactly what Gould attacks as "just-so stories." Politically, the book has been a target of the left for decades for "providing a biological excuse for social inequality." That debate is not over, and this book is one of its starting points.

Symbiotic Planet
Symbiotic Planet: A New Look at Evolution · Lynn Margulis · 1998
Basic Books · about 170 pages · Science Masters series
A small book of only 170 pages, with an outsized impact on evolutionary theory. Margulis's lifework — endosymbiotic theory — was rejected by the mainstream for over a decade in the 1960s–70s; this is her retrospective summary written after acceptance.
Core claims
  • Symbiogenesis is the main source of evolutionary novelty. New tissues, organs, and species arise mainly not by accumulation of point mutations but through long-term symbiotic relationships that fuse. This is a fundamental correction to the "gradual accumulation" mainstream narrative.
  • The eukaryotic cell is itself a merger. Mitochondria descend from engulfed but undigested aerobic bacteria; chloroplasts descend from engulfed cyanobacteria. About 90% of the cells in your body are descendants of bacteria — a human is not "an individual" but an ecosystem.
  • The Modern Synthesis ignored the microbial layer. Mendel + Darwin's modern synthesis operated mostly at the macro-eukaryote level (plants and animals) and largely overlooked the actual evolutionary mechanisms of the microbial world (horizontal gene transfer, symbiotic fusion). The 21st-century "Extended Synthesis" brings these back into view.
  • Cooperation, networking, and interdependence are the real path by which life took over the planet — not the absence of competition, but competition is only the surface. Underneath, the dominant mode is cooperation. Margulis uses "Darwinism reduced to bloody competition" as a recurring target.
Notable quotes
"Life did not take over the globe by combat, but by networking."
— Margulis's signature line, restated in the Introduction and Chapter 1 of Symbiotic Planet
"I am claiming that most evolutionary novelty arose, and still arises, directly from symbiosis."
— Symbiotic Planet, Chapter 2 "Power Tactics"
Conversation with the other three

This is the most fundamental challenge to the "gene competition" narrative of Dawkins and Wilson. Margulis does not deny natural selection, but she insists that selection acts on already-merged composites — the single-gene view cannot see the merger event itself. Natural ally with Gould: both emphasize evolutionary mechanisms beyond gene-reductionism and both reject the totalizing reach of adaptationism; Gould explicitly praises Margulis in Wonderful Life. With Dawkins's famous "extended phenotype" idea there is a quiet long-distance dialogue — Margulis would say: "Your extension is of the gene's phenotype, mine is the merger of the cells themselves."

Limits and controversy

Margulis's doubts about HIV causing AIDS and parts of her sharp critiques of mainstream Darwinism stirred controversy. Read her here by separating her core contribution (endosymbiosis) from some of her late-career positions — the former is textbook material; the latter remain contested. The prose mixes personal memoir and scientific exposition; the structure is loose, not a textbook-style systematic presentation. Strength: personal stories. Weakness: a first-time reader may have trouble extracting the argumentative spine. Endosymbiosis is now high-school biology, so today's reader may feel the book argues an obvious point — but remember the resistance it faced between 1967 and 1985.

The Panda's Thumb
The Panda's Thumb: More Reflections in Natural History · Stephen Jay Gould · 1980
W. W. Norton · about 350 pages · National Book Critics Circle Award, 1981
The second collection of Gould's Natural History magazine columns. Thirty-one essays of 1,500–2,500 words; each uses a specific biological oddity to illuminate a large theoretical question.
Core claims
  • Odd structures are the best evidence of evolution. The panda's "thumb" is actually a sesamoid bone in the wrist remodeled into a pseudo-thumb. If designed, no engineer would do it this way; precisely because it is a makeshift repair constrained by historical path, the "imperfection" is exactly what proves the reality of evolution. This is the argument of the title essay.
  • Punctuated equilibrium. Gould and Eldredge in 1972 proposed that species spend most of their existence in morphological stasis, with the main evolutionary events concentrated in short bursts of speciation rather than slow uniform accumulation. This stands in direct opposition to Darwinian gradualism.
  • Not every trait is an adaptation. Many morphological features are "spandrels" — byproducts of some other adaptation, not independently selected. Gould and Lewontin's 1979 "Spandrels of San Marco" paper is the manifesto for this position; this book is its popular-science elaboration.
  • Evolution has no "progressive" direction. Human cognitive complexity is not evolution's "goal" — it is the product of historical contingency plus structural opportunity. Replay the tape of life and the outcome will almost certainly look entirely different. This argument is systematically developed in Wonderful Life (1989); the present book is its early form.
Notable quotes
"Odd arrangements and funny solutions are the proof of evolution—paths that a sensible God would never tread but that a natural process, constrained by history, follows perforce."
— The Panda's Thumb, title essay "The Panda's Thumb"
"Ideal design is a lousy argument for evolution, for it mimics the postulated action of an omnipotent creator."
— same essay
Conversation with the other three

This is the head-on attack on the adaptationism of Dawkins and Wilson. In Dawkins's gene-centric frame, any stable trait is by default the "extended phenotype" of some gene; Gould asks: "How do you know it is not a spandrel — a byproduct dragged along by some other adaptation?" His sharpest fire is reserved for sociobiology's "just-so stories" (where every behavior gets narrated as an adaptation). Natural ally with Margulis: both reject single-mechanism evolutionary explanations and both insist on the importance of "middle-layer" mechanisms — development, history, ecology. The difference: Margulis's middle layer is cellular symbiosis; Gould's is morphological constraint and historical path.

Limits and controversy

The punctuated-equilibrium debate ran very hot in the 1980s and 1990s; Dawkins and Maynard Smith argued it was less novel than Gould claimed, just a special rate of gradualist selection, not the "non-Darwinian" departure Gould framed. The spandrel concept has been criticized as verbally slippery — it is hard to draw a sharp line between an adaptation and a byproduct. Gould's prose is itself debate-shaped; his portrayals of opponents are not always fully fair (Dawkins protested this repeatedly). But as a sober antidote to pure selectionism, this book holds up — it teaches you to ask "really?" before accepting any "X exists because X is adaptive" story.

Reading order

Path A · Classic confrontation (recommended)

Dawkins → Wilson → Gould → Margulis. Start with Dawkins to set up the cleanest extreme of gene-centrism. Then Wilson to see how it extends into human society. Then Gould for the strongest pushback from paleontology. Finally Margulis for a completely different dimension of "expansion" (symbiosis). This order also moves from the smallest to the largest scale.

Path B · If you have time for only two

Dawkins + Gould. One pro, one con — together they form the most charged pair in 20th-century evolutionary popular science. Dawkins gives you the sharpest frame; Gould gives you the strongest immunity to it. After these two, any sociobiology or evo-psych article will trigger an extra layer of caution.

Path C · Philosophy first

Gould → Margulis → Dawkins → Wilson. Begin with Gould to install a "pluralistic mechanism" baseline. Then Margulis to see the overlooked force of symbiosis. Then read Dawkins's gene-centric extreme — you will find that the word "selfish" no longer rattles you. Finish with Wilson to see the concrete consequences of gene-centrism applied to humans, and judge for yourself whether that step is warranted.

Path D · Microbe first

Margulis → Dawkins → Wilson → Gould. If your interest is in the biological substrate rather than in human behavior, start with Margulis — you will read Dawkins afterward carrying the fact that "the eukaryotic cell is a merger," and the blind spots in his account jump out. End with Gould, whose polemics often target Wilson — by then you will be primed to enjoy the showdown.

Questions you can ask yourself after

  1. When you hear someone explain "human behavior X was selected by evolution," can you tell whether the argument is in the Dawkins–Wilson lineage or is a Gould-style "just-so story" that he would reject?
  2. In your own working environment (team, organization, community), which narrative is more often taken as default — competition or cooperation / symbiosis? What does each narrative miss?
  3. "The selfish gene" has entered popular culture as a metaphor used to explain family, business, and politics. Where does the popular reading diverge from what Dawkins actually said in context?
  4. If you accept Margulis's view, what happens to the concept of "individual"? Are you willing to understand yourself as "a composite of bacterial descendants and merged cells"? Does that view shake your intuition about the boundary of self?
  5. Gould's "replay the tape of life" thought experiment: rerun Earth's history, and humans almost certainly would not exist. Does this make you uneasy? Why? What does that unease reveal about your default assumptions about "evolution"?

Further reading

  • The Extended Phenotype · Richard Dawkins (1982) — Dawkins's own favorite of his books. Extends gene-centrism from the body to behavioral products, artifacts, and parasite manipulation. The natural next step for anyone hooked by The Selfish Gene.
  • Wonderful Life: The Burgess Shale and the Nature of History · Stephen Jay Gould (1989) — Gould's systematic case for historical contingency. From the Burgess Shale fossils in Canada to the "replay the tape" thought experiment. More focused and more philosophical than The Panda's Thumb.
  • Sociobiology: The New Synthesis · E. O. Wilson (1975) — The prequel to On Human Nature. A 750-page tome; the first 26 chapters survey animal social behavior, and Chapter 27 pushes the lens to humans — the chapter that detonated the entire controversy.
  • What Is Life? · Lynn Margulis & Dorion Sagan (1995) — Margulis and her son's answer to the title's question. Larger in scope than Symbiotic Planet; places endosymbiosis inside a broader frame of "life as an integrated planetary phenomenon."
  • Darwin's Dangerous Idea · Daniel Dennett (1995) — A philosopher's defense of the Dawkins frame, plus systematic counter-arguments against Gould's "punctuated equilibrium" and "spandrels." Read after this issue, and you get the "Dawkins-camp reply book."
  • Evolution: The Extended Synthesis · ed. Pigliucci & Müller (2010) — A representative academic collection from the 21st-century attempt to integrate the Margulis–Gould revisionists with the New Synthesis. The "current state" report on the debate this issue records.
