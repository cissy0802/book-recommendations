Indian Thought

Peel off the "spiritual East" label: what is the real shape of a civilization that pushed inwardness to its limit, yet was every bit as argumentative, plural, and self-contradicting?

2026 · Book Recommendations · Issue 37

Introduction

"India = the spiritual East" is a line repeated far too often. The four books here each grab a distinct mechanism. The Upanishads push "who am I?" all the way down to the claim that the individual self and the ground of the universe are one and the same. The Bhagavad Gita answers the next question — "given that, should one still act?" — with: act fully, and release your grip on the results. Sen's The Argumentative Indian recovers the neglected other half: India is equally a tradition of argument, reasoning, and rampant heterodoxy. Doniger's The Hindus: An Alternative History dismantles the "single orthodoxy," letting the many Hinduisms of the women, the outcastes, and the folk — silenced by the mainstream narrative — step forward.

The Four Books in Detail

The Upanishads
Anonymous (Vedic sages) · c. 800–200 BCE
Eknath Easwaran translation, Nilgiri Press · the dozen-odd principal Upanishads
The source code of Indian metaphysics — driving "who am I?" clear through until the individual self and the ground of the whole universe turn out to be one and the same thing.
The Core Insight

The Upanishads (Upaniṣad, literally "sitting near" — the disciple seated close at the teacher's knee to receive the hidden teaching) are the water table of Indian thought. Early Vedic religion was still offering sacrifices to the gods, praying for rain and sons; by the time of the Upanishads the whole line of questioning has turned around — from "how to please an external god" to probing inward: who am I, really? That turn is the deepest inward move in the history of Indian thought.

The core insight compresses into a single equation: Ātman is Brahman. Brahman is the one, infinite, changeless ground behind the universe; Ātman is the deepest "true Self" left once you strip away body, emotion, thought, and identity. The Upanishads' astonishing claim is that the two are not merely similar but the very same thing — the innermost "I" and the ultimate reality of the whole cosmos are one. The Chāndogya Upanishad's line, which a father repeats to his son — "tat tvam asi (that thou art)" — says exactly this.

How does one reach that true Self? The method the Upanishads give is a stripping-away, layer by layer: "neti, neti (not this, not this)." Am I the body? No — the body changes and dies, yet the one aware of it remains. Am I the emotions, the thoughts, my name and roles? None of these — they come and go, while the awareness watching them come and go never stirs. Negate every "object that can be perceived," one by one, and what is left — that pure witness that can never be made into an object — is Ātman. It is a metaphysics of subtraction: the true Self cannot be grasped, only approached by negating everything it is not.

The weight of this thought lies in its answering three things at once: what reality is (Brahman), what I am (Ātman), and what liberation is (moksha — recognizing the two as one, waking from the illusion of the cycle of birth and death). Nearly every later Indian school of philosophy, and even the Buddhist debate over "no-self," is in dialogue or in combat with the Upanishads. Without first touching this water table, any later Indian thought reads like a stream with no source.

Key Quotes
"That thou art."
— Chāndogya Upanishad 6.8.7 (the father's core teaching to his son)
"Lead me from the unreal to the real, from darkness to light, from death to immortality."
— Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upanishad 1.3.28
"Not this, not this."
— Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upanishad 2.3.6 (the only way to describe the true Self: only by negation, never by assertion)
Limits

The Upanishads were composed over centuries by many hands; they are not internally consistent, and read as contradictory, cryptic, and jumpy — a body of flashing dialogues and aphorisms, not a systematic philosophy. "Ātman is Brahman" is also easily flattened into a cheap "oneness with the cosmos" bromide that drops the severity of the negation and the actual practice.

For BigCat

Given your deep interest in consciousness, the Upanishads are an unavoidable source — and what they offer is not theory but a method. This week, try a 10-minute "neti neti" self-inquiry: sit, and ask in turn, "Am I this body? Am I this emotion right now? Am I this thought? Am I this identity of 'AI super-individual'?" — and with each question, notice that the very awareness perceiving them is never among the things being negated. You need not believe any metaphysical conclusion; treat it as one phenomenological observation: practice shifting attention from "the perceived content" to "awareness itself." This is precisely where the Upanishads meet, head-on, the discussions of the "self-model" you know from Buddhism and neuroscience.

The Bhagavad Gita
An interlude in the Mahābhārata · composed c. 2nd c. BCE
Eknath Easwaran translation, Nilgiri Press · 18 chapters, 700 verses
If the deepest true Self never dies, why should one still labor to act? The Gita gives Indian thought's most famous answer: act fully, then release your grip on the fruits.
The Core Insight

The scene sits at the very center of the epic Mahābhārata: on the eve of a great war, the prince Arjuna stands between the two armies, sees his own kin and teachers on the opposing side, and utterly breaks down — he throws down his bow and says he will not fight; what is the point of a victory won by killing one's family? The whole Bhagavad Gita ("Song of the Lord") is the counsel his charioteer, the god Krishna in human form, gives him. It turns a moral collapse into a lesson on how a person should, in the end, act.

Krishna's central prescription is "karma yoga" — action without attachment: you have a right to the action itself, but never to its fruits. To renounce action is cowardice, and impossible anyway (to be alive is to act); but fixing the heart on the outcome — success or failure, reward, the judgment of others — is precisely the root of suffering. The solution is not to stop acting, but to act with all your strength while wholly releasing your grip on the result, treating the action as an offering rather than a transaction. This stitches together two things that look irreconcilable: the world-renouncing pursuit of liberation and the worldly discharge of duty.

Three yogic paths · one liberation

The Gita's further ambition is to unify the several roads to liberation: the path of knowledge (jñāna, through insight into the true Self), the path of action (karma, through action without attachment), and the path of devotion (bhakti, through wholehearted love of God). It ranks none above the others, saying instead that these are different doors suited to different people, all pointing to the same liberation. It is exactly this inclusiveness that made a mere 700-verse booklet the most widely embraced scripture of all Hinduism — ascetic and farmer alike could each take what they needed.

Worth noting are two of its modern echoes. Gandhi read the Gita as a manual of nonviolence and selfless action (he called it his "spiritual dictionary") and, on that basis, launched a movement that changed India; while Oppenheimer, who built the atomic bomb, found rising in his mind at the moment of the first successful nuclear test the line from the Gita's Chapter 11, as Krishna reveals his cosmic form — "I am Time, the destroyer of worlds." The same book: one man reads redemption in it, the other destruction — which is just to say that what it touches is fundamental enough.

Key Quotes
"You have a right to your actions, but never to the fruits of your actions; act not for the reward, nor cling to inaction."
— Bhagavad Gita 2.47 (the core of karma yoga)
"It is never born, and it never dies … when the body is slain, it is not slain."
— Bhagavad Gita 2.20 (on the imperishable true Self)
"Whenever dharma declines and lawlessness rises, I take birth, age after age."
— Bhagavad Gita 4.7
Limits

The Gita was born in the context of justifying, for the Kshatriya (warrior caste), the discharge of "duty on the battlefield"; the doctrine of "act according to your caste-duty" (svadharma) has historically also been used to underwrite the caste system and war — it must be read apart from its wisdom on liberation. As a philosophical poem inserted into an epic, its deliberate reconciliation of many schools comes at the cost of systematic rigor.

For BigCat

Krishna's "a right to the action, none to the fruit" is almost a tailor-made antidote for the modern creator chased by metrics. The daily life of an AI super-individual is exposed to the sharpest "fruit-anxiety": view counts, likes, star counts, model benchmarks, others' judgments — the moment the heart fixes on these numbers, the action deforms. Try this week: pick one project you must ship this week, and before you start, write down one sentence defining "what it means to have done it well" — and that judgment must contain no external-reaction metric whatsoever (not "how many likes," but "is the argument honest, is it thoroughly explained"). When done, measure only against that self-assessment, and deliberately do not rush to check the numbers. This is not passivity — karma yoga demands precisely a more committed doing, only with the heart untied from the "fruit."

The Argumentative Indian
The Argumentative Indian: Writings on Indian History, Culture and Identity · Amartya Sen · 2005
Farrar, Straus and Giroux / Penguin · ~432 pages
If the first two books display India's "inward, spiritual" face, Sen insists on recovering the other half that label buries: India is equally one of the world's most flourishing civilizations of argument, reasoning, and heterodoxy.
The Core Insight

Amartya Sen is a Nobel laureate in economics, but this book is his act of vindication as an Indian. What he sets out to dismantle is exactly the stereotype the West (and some Indians themselves) read out of the Upanishads and the Gita: India = mystical, transcendent, all spirituality and no reasoning — the "spiritual East." That, Sen says, hacks off a large half of India's enormously rich intellectual tradition.

The half he recovers is the "argumentative" tradition: India has an extremely long history of public debate, skepticism, heterodoxy and rational reasoning. Here are the Cārvāka, who openly professed materialism and denied an afterlife or a soul; the woman philosopher Gārgī, who publicly cross-examined a sage in a grand debate; schools that wrote atheism into the canon. Even the Bhagavad Gita, Sen reminds you, is itself an argument — Arjuna's doubt (is this slaughter really right?) is never truly refuted by Krishna; it is left as an open question for the reader.

Sen's deeper argument carries a present-day concern: this long-lived tradition of public argument is the intellectual soil of Indian democracy. Democracy is no British import; it connects to India's own native root of "settling disagreement through reasoning and dissent" — Ashoka's edicts of religious tolerance and the emperor Akbar's cross-faith debates are its forerunners. Whether one can have voice, can reason in public, is the core mechanism of social justice — and a weapon against inequality and poverty.

This book is therefore the best counterweight to the first two: the spiritual India is real, but reading India as only spiritual is both a Western Orientalist misreading and something India's own religious nationalism can seize — using a single, pure, unarguable "Indianness" to crush plurality. Sen's remedy is plain: remember that you have always been argumentative.

Key Quotes
"Prolixity is not alien to us in India. We are able to talk at some length."
— The Argumentative Indian, opening lines
"The argumentative tradition, if used with deliberation and commitment, can also be extremely important in resisting social inequalities and in removing poverty and deprivation."
— The Argumentative Indian, title essay "The Argumentative Indian"
Limits

Sen's selection of evidence for the "argumentative tradition" is not free of the suspicion of cherry-picking for the thesis — he stresses the tolerant-of-dissent side, giving lighter treatment to the equally long-standing orthodox repression and caste violence in the same tradition. Assembled from many essays, the book is somewhat scattered in theme, and parts are aimed at India's domestic political context, which outside readers cannot fully enter.

For BigCat

What is most transferable in Sen is treating "argument" as a discipline against the single, certain answer — especially sharp for you, who use AI every day. A large model's default failure mode is to hand you an answer in a fluent, confident tone, luring you to stop asking. Try this week: for any judgment you have AI help you make (a tech choice, an investment thesis, a viewpoint), do not accept its first-pass conclusion; instead force it to open an Indian-style debate — first have it take the stance of a "Cārvāka" and give the strongest opposing case, then let the affirmative reply, with you as the sole judge. Demote AI from "an oracle handing down answers" to "an opponent you argue with," and the quality of your judgment will noticeably differ. This is exactly Sen's "settling disagreement through public reasoning," carried into human–machine collaboration.

The Hindus: An Alternative History
The Hindus: An Alternative History · Wendy Doniger · 2009
The Penguin Press · ~779 pages
The earlier books mostly recount the "great tradition" of the Sanskrit classics. Doniger insists on writing an "alternative history": there was never one Hinduism, only the many Hinduisms silenced by the mainstream narrative.
The Core Insight

Wendy Doniger is a leading Sanskrit scholar of Hinduism. Her nearly 800-page book announces itself in its subtitle, "An Alternative History": this is "a" history of the Hindus, not "the" history. Mainstream tellings always center the Brahmins, the Sanskrit classics, the male elite; Doniger insists on turning the lens toward those silenced by that orthodoxy — women, outcastes (Dalits), tribal peoples, and even the animals in the myths.

Her central thesis: there has never existed a singular, unified, pure "that Hinduism." The so-called orthodoxy is a later product — constructed, pruned, and standardized, especially in the colonial period and by modern nationalism. The real history of Hinduism is plural, self-contradicting, a jungle teeming with the folk, the bodily, the erotic, and rule-breaking myth — not a tidy temple. What she means to restore is exactly this flattened-out diversity and mess.

Methodologically, Doniger excels at reading against the grain of the classics: from the same myth, orthodoxy reads a sacred order, while she reads the repressed female desire, the caste anxiety, the mockery and subversion of established rules within it. This has made her deeply controversial — in 2014, under pressure from a Hindu-nationalist group, her Indian publisher pulped and recalled the book. That very furor became the best footnote to her argument: why should the question of "who has the right to narrate Hinduism" enrage people to the point of burning books?

Within this issue she is the necessary final piece: if the Upanishads and the Gita give you the peaks of "Indian thought" and Sen gives you the skeleton of "argument," then Doniger reminds you that even these classics are themselves the product of selection and interpretation — behind every "tradition" lies a struggle over "whose version counts." It is a sobering shot against any "single grand narrative."

Key Quotes
"This is a history, not the history, of the Hindus."
— The Hindus: An Alternative History, Introduction (the crux of the word "alternative")
Limits

Doniger's "reading against the grain" is full of insight, but her strong psychoanalytic and erotic lens sometimes projects modern questions too far back onto ancient texts, drawing the charge of "letting the thesis drive the history." At nearly 800 pages with many digressions, it sets a high bar for the first-time reader of Hinduism — it is more a veteran's "alternative supplement" than a first primer.

For BigCat

Doniger's method can be abstracted into a general thinking tool: facing any "official version," first ask, "whose voice did this version delete?" This is useful for you, who work in complex systems and also make investment judgments — any clean, self-consistent, widely circulated narrative (that some AI technology will inevitably win, that some market must go a certain way, that a stretch of tech history "just developed like this") is almost always written by the survivors and the winners, having erased the failed branches, the marginalized paths, the silenced data. Try this week: pick one "standard story" you currently hold as certain, and spend an hour deliberately reconstructing its "alternative history" — who has no place in this story? If the deleted side told it, what would the same history become? Very often, the real opportunity and the real risk hide in exactly the half the mainstream narrative silenced.

Questions to Ask Yourself

  1. Which of Arjuna's two paralyses are you closer to right now: unable to think through "who am I, really?" (the Upanishads' problem), or knowing full well what to do yet frozen by "how will the result turn out?" (the Gita's problem)?
    A frame for judging

    The two paralyses need different medicines. The first (spinning in place at the level of identity and meaning) needs the Upanishads' inward look — loosening, one by one, the provisional labels of "I am my title / achievement / role," to first tell apart which is the anxious "I." The second (knowing what to do yet stalling out of fear of failure) needs karma yoga — cleaving "doing this well" cleanly from "how this will be judged," so that you answer only for the former. Tell first which layer you are stuck in, and you won't reach for the wrong medicine.

  2. The last time you truly disagreed with a consensus (in your team, in a judgment about AI), did you make the dissent clear in the open, or politely swallow it?
    A frame for judging

    Sen's point is that a healthy system relies not on an absence of disagreement, but on disagreement's being laid out in the open, by reasoning. Check the environments you inhabit (team, family, your dialogue with AI): is dissent encouraged into speech, or quietly flattened by default? One concrete signal — how long has it been since you deliberately played the "strongest opposition" in a decision? If the answer is "a long time," you may be trading a surface harmony for a quiet decline in the quality of your judgment.

  3. That "standard story" you hold as beyond doubt — who wrote it? Whom did it delete?
    A frame for judging

    A passing self-examination must be able to name names. Pick one narrative you currently hold as certain (some technology path is bound to win, some stretch of history "was meant to be," some person "is just like that"), and ask three things: (1) Whom does this version most benefit? (2) If the story's losers / the silenced told it, which part would be rewritten? (3) Do I believe it because of evidence, or because it is told cleanly and sounds smooth? Doniger's reminder is exactly this: the tidier, more singular, more unquestionable a narrative, the more likely it has been pruned.
